# NASA publishes limits and feeds, not vitals

No space agency releases individual astronaut health readings, so the readings behind all 13 of Astro's indicators have to stay simulated and labelled; what NASA does publish, and what a static page can use before Sunday November 15 at 11:59 PM Central, is the layer around those readings. Three kinds of material fit. NASA's numeric standards and published reference values cover **7 of the 13 indicators** and can ship as one small bundled file with no network dependency. Browser checks on November 14 confirmed **live, key-free reads** of the DONKI space weather database (on its CCMC host), NOAA's GOES proton feed, the NASA Image and Video Library and SSCWeb from the app's own GitHub Pages origin, and showed the ISS cabin telemetry stream running live on another GitHub Pages site. The measured datasets with the strongest scientific fit, OSDR's RadLab dose rates and the Inspiration4 blood panels, **failed the same browser test** and can be used only as files saved into the repository, with contents nobody has yet confirmed. This matters because the 2026 Relevance criterion reserves its top score for projects where NASA data is a core component, while today NASA material gives Astro its framework and two alert limits. Ranked by fit, effort and risk, the options run from a bundled NASA reference layer (2 to 4 hours, low risk), through a DONKI-driven radiation alert (3 to 4 hours) and a live ISS cabin panel (2 to 4 hours), to the RadLab and Inspiration4 snapshots, which carry the most weight as datasets and the most uncertainty. Partner agencies (CSA, ESA, JAXA) supply citations and a few constants, not data feeds. What to build from this list is the team's call; this report sets out the evidence and the trade-offs for each option.

## No agency releases crew vitals, so Astro's readings stay simulated

NASA already holds the records Astro imitates. Its Lifetime Surveillance of Astronaut Health programme keeps annual exams with vital signs and medications, bone density scans, blood work and urinalysis, behavioural health assessments, radiation exposure evaluations and in-flight monitoring for a cohort of 360 astronauts ([NTRS 20260001897](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20260001897/downloads/Cohort%20Profile%20Manuscript%2003032026.pdf)). None of it can be downloaded. A 2017 briefing by the programme's manager explains that individual astronaut data falls under the Privacy Act and reaches researchers only through a working group, an advisory board, an ethics review, crew consent and a data use agreement, with **each stage quoted in months** (review and approval alone takes two to six) ([NTRS 20170009502](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20170009502/downloads/20170009502.pdf)). The archive's public face, the NASA Life Sciences Portal, is a searchable catalogue with no documented API or bulk export, and its data.nasa.gov entry is a link to a web page, not a file ([data.nasa.gov NLSP entry](https://data.nasa.gov/dataset/nasa-life-sciences-portal-nlsp)). The programme's own publications are newsletters and aggregate cohort statistics ([NASA LSAH newsletter page](https://www.nasa.gov/general/lsah-newsletter)). The NASA Twins Study sits behind the same request process ([Garrett-Bakelman et al. 2019](https://lsom.uthscsa.edu/nephrology/wp-content/uploads/sites/38/2019/04/NASA-twin-study_Science.pdf)), Spaceflight Standard Measures publishes its measurement panel but no numbers ([NASA Standard Measures](https://www.nasa.gov/reference/standard-measures/)), and NASA's medical risk models expose only their size: 100 conditions in the Integrated Medical Model and 120 in its exploration successor ([NTRS 20205008590](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20205008590); [Kreykes et al. 2023](https://asma.kglmeridian.com/view/journals/amhp/94/7/article-p550.xml)).

The Open Science Data Repository (OSDR) holds one open exception. Ten Inspiration4 studies (OSD-569 to OSD-575, OSD-630, OSD-656 and OSD-687) cover four civilian crew on a three-day flight, with processed tables public and raw sequence data behind a controlled-access application ([NASA I4 datasets](https://www.nasa.gov/osdr-latest-news-i4-mission-datasets/); [npj Microgravity 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41526-024-00393-5)). The clinical tables include a complete blood count and metabolic and cytokine panels, and a third-party dashboard reads the blood count from OSD-569 across seven time points with reference-range columns ([Triple-T dashboard](https://torchlight-summit.pages.dev/narrative/hematologic/)). Which accession holds the metabolic panel, whether it lists glucose and potassium, and in what units are all unconfirmed, because the study pages render only in a full browser. No in-flight blood chemistry is reported in the sources opened, so these tables give before-and-after snapshots of four people, not a mission time series. The only other astronaut dataset, JAXA's cell-free RNA study of six astronauts (OSD-530), is omics with no bearing on any indicator ([Nature Communications 2024](https://link.springer.com/10.1038/s41467-023-41995-z)). The researchers found no OSDR source for heart rate, blood pressure, temperature, body mass, aerobic capacity, vitamin D, urine volume, urinary calcium, sleep or mood, although the repository's search API could not be queried to prove the absence.

Partner agencies follow the same rule. CSA describes its Bio-Monitor smart shirt, its Space Health study of ten astronauts and its Vascular series in detail and releases no data from any of them ([CSA Bio-Monitor](https://www.asc-csa.gc.ca/eng/sciences/bio-monitor.asp); [CSA Space Health](https://asc-csa.gc.ca/eng/sciences/space-flight-improve-monitor-health-earth.asp); [CSA Vascular](https://www.asc-csa.gc.ca/eng/sciences/vascular.asp)). ESA's archive demands a login to download any science data, public data included ([HREDA Introduction](https://hreda.esac.esa.int/hreda-help/introduction/index.html)), and its pooled bed rest data went out through a call that has closed ([ESA AO-2023-BedrestCoreData](https://scispace.esa.int/research-opportunities/ao-2023-bedrestcoredata)). JAXA lists every astronaut flight that carried a personal dosimeter and publishes none of the personal doses ([JAXA Crew PADLES](https://humans-in-space.jaxa.jp/spacerad/EI003.html)). A 2025 paper by ISS partner agency staff states the principle outright: crew medical data is confidential and reusable only for the investigation the crew member consented to, and ASI's human physiology database opens only to a research proposal with ethics approval ([IAC-25-B3-9](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20250008980/downloads/Open%20data%20article-PSF-IAC-25-B3-9.pdf)).

The operational gaps are just as firm. The public ISS telemetry stream carries no crew sleep or wake state, no crew timeline, no humidity, noise, trace contaminant or radiation channel and no personal measurement of any kind ([ISSLive symbol list](https://github.com/Lightstreamer/Lightstreamer-example-ISSLive-client-javascript/blob/master/src/assets/PUIList.xml)). The api.nasa.gov catalogue lists 16 APIs and none returns crew health, cabin environment or medication data ([api.nasa.gov catalogue source](https://github.com/nasa/api-docs/blob/gh-pages/assets/json/apis.json)); searches of data.nasa.gov surfaced one crew activity table, a spacewalk log last modified in January 2023, and no cabin air, water, acoustic or crew health time series ([data.nasa.gov EVA dataset](https://data.nasa.gov/dataset/extra-vehicular-activity-eva-us-and-russia)). Even the three NASA Human Research Program pages in the challenge's own resource list are explainers that link to no dataset ([NASA HRP Hazards](https://www.nasa.gov/hrp/hazards/); [NASA HRP Risks](https://www.nasa.gov/hrp/risks/)).

Simulation is therefore the only design open to any team, and the live 2026 submission guide, read in a logged-in browser on November 13 and 14, turns that into a disclosure duty by requiring simulated data to be labelled ([Project Submission Guide](https://www.spaceappschallenge.org/resources/project-submission-guide/)). The useful consequence is that NASA's public description of what flight medicine tracks matches Astro's indicator set closely, and Standard Measures uses the same before, during and after structure that the app uses for baselines. Astro can state both facts truthfully without importing a single reading.

## NASA numbers exist for seven of the thirteen indicators

The standards behind those numbers are current: OCHMO's standards page lists NASA-STD-3001 Volume 1 at Revision C (September 2023) and Volume 2 at Revision F, approved on July 14, 2026 ([OCHMO standards page](https://www.nasa.gov/ochmo/human-spaceflight-and-aviation-standards)). Sorting the indicators against these documents and NASA's evidence reports produces three tiers. A formal NASA standard exists for **two indicators** (aerobic capacity and cumulative radiation dose), a NASA-published reference value exists for **five more** (vitamin D, urine volume, urinary calcium, sleep and body mass), and for **six** (resting heart rate, blood pressure, body temperature, fasting glucose, blood potassium and mood) NASA publishes no numeric in-flight threshold at all. Astro's two NASA-labelled limits hold up exactly: the 80 percent aerobic floor is requirement [V1 4003] and the 600 mSv career limit is [V1 4030] of Volume 1 Revision C. The aerobic floor is also a live issue in orbit, since NASA's exercise brief reports that **12 to 21 percent of crew members fell below it** at in-flight test points ([OCHMO-TB-031](https://www.nasa.gov/ochmo-tb-031-exercise-overview-2/)). The app's other five thresholds sit close to NASA's numbers without matching them, and each mismatch is a design choice for the team. NASA's bone loss brief sets the desired vitamin D range at 35 to 90 ng/mL, so a reading of 32 passes Astro's 30 ng/mL watch line while falling short of NASA's range ([OCHMO-MTB-013](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/ochmo-mtb-013-bone-loss.pdf)). The renal stone evidence report treats 2 to 2.5 L/day of urine as normal and places the rise in stone risk below 1.5 L/day, a line that falls between Astro's 2.0 and 1.0 marks; the same report uses 250 and 300 mg/day of urinary calcium as the reference limits for women and men, where Astro uses them as two severity steps ([NTRS 20170004709](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20170004709/downloads/20170004709.pdf)). Body mass flags at 5 and 10 percent reproduce the bands NASA's nutrition book reports for ISS crews, though the standard itself sets no body mass limit, so "categories NASA reports" is the accurate label and "NASA limit" is not ([NASA nutrition book](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2015/02/human_adaptation_2021_final.pdf)).

Two of these comparisons carry a warning about alert fatigue. NASA recommends an 8.5-hour sleep period and measures 6 to 6.5 hours, so a realistically simulated crew member sits under Astro's 7-hour watch line on most nights and close to the 6-hour attention line on many ([OCHMO-TB-041](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ochmo-tb-041-sleep-accommodations.pdf)). With 57 percent of ISS astronauts in the 5 to 10 percent loss band at their lowest point, the body mass watch flag likewise describes the typical mission, not an exception. Flags that fire on normal spaceflight adaptation are accurate against Earth norms and weak as triage signals. Whether to key them to a personal trend, to NASA's measured in-flight norms, or to leave them as they are is a judgment the evidence cannot settle for the team.

For the six indicators with no NASA threshold, the standard's own language supports what Astro already does: behavioural state is judged against clinically accepted values [V1 4011], and hematology and immunology values against normative ranges for the healthy general population [V1 4015] ([NASA-STD-3001 Vol 1 Rev C](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/nasa-std-3001-vol-1-rev-c-with-signature.pdf)). The in-flight studies add direction, not limits. The clearest case is temperature: resting core temperature climbed about 1 °C over the first 2.5 months on the ISS in a German-led study with NASA co-authors, which means an Earth fever cut-off would misfire in orbit and a personal baseline is the defensible display ([Stahn et al. 2017](https://air.unimi.it/bitstream/2434/533558/2/ScientificReports_IncreasedCoreBodyTemperature_2017.pdf)). That study belongs under other references, not NASA data.

One caution applies to every figure in this section. The researchers read these documents through a tool that returns extracted text, so each number needs a look at the source PDF before it appears in the app. Two specific loose ends are the subject count behind the body mass percentages (the figure caption lists 61 astronauts where the text says 79) and the widely quoted 6.09-hour ISS sleep average, known here only through a press release ([Brigham and Women's Hospital release](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/595450)).

### What is live

The ISS feed is the only live measurement from a crewed vehicle that a static page can reach. NASA released a public subset of station telemetry in 2011 and delivers it through Lightstreamer's server, which a browser subscribes to with no key ([ISS Mimic README](https://github.com/ISS-Mimic/Mimic/blob/main/README.md); [Lightstreamer ISSLive example](https://github.com/Lightstreamer/Lightstreamer-example-ISSLive-client-javascript/blob/master/README.md)). ISS Mimic showed an acquired signal at 21:48 GMT on November 14 with same-day values of **758.25 torr cabin pressure and 23.76 °C cabin temperature**, plus urine, waste water and clean water tank levels ([ISS Mimic live page](https://iss-mimic.github.io/Mimic/)). That settles a unit conflict between the feed's 2011 symbol dictionary (psi) and current dashboards in favour of torr and degrees Celsius. Both readings can be checked live against NASA's habitat requirements: the temperature falls inside the 18 to 27 °C health range [V2 6012] and the 20 to 25 °C performance zone [V2 6013], and the pressure, about 101 kPa by conversion, sits within the 34.5 to 103 kPa band for indefinite exposure [V2 6006] ([OCHMO-TB-002](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ochmo-tb-002-eclss.pdf)).

The gas channels tell a different story. Oxygen, nitrogen and carbon dioxide partial pressures in the Lab and Node 3 had last updated on GMT day 306 or 307, 11 to 12 days before the test, with carbon dioxide frozen at 2.44 torr in the Lab and 2.95 torr in Node 3. The most attractive comparison on paper, live carbon dioxide against NASA's 3 mmHg one-hour limit [V2 6004] ([OCHMO-TB-004](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ochmo-tb-004-carbon-dioxide.pdf)), is therefore **not available as a live check this weekend**; the values can be shown only with their age. ISS Mimic's maintainers document exactly this behaviour: signal drops on every orbit and at relay handovers, and stale periods lasting from minutes to a couple of weeks when the public adapters in Mission Control stop updating ([ISS Mimic wiki](https://github.com/ISS-Mimic/Mimic/wiki/ISS-Telemetry)). Any panel built on the feed needs a fresh, lost or stale state per value, the last timestamp and a bundled fallback. It also describes the ISS only, so in Moon or Mars mission modes it is an analog and not that vehicle's cabin. No NASA terms of use for the stream were found, and the client library loads from Lightstreamer's demo server unless it is copied into the repository.

Space weather is the cleanest live source. DONKI answered from the CCMC host in 233 ms with no key, while the api.nasa.gov route to the same database returned a 404, so the CCMC host is the one to use. The records are real and timestamped: a query for May 2024 returns 14 solar energetic particle events between May 9 and May 13, alongside an X5.8 flare and a geomagnetic storm that reached Kp 9 ([DONKI particle events, May 2024](https://ccmc.gsfc.nasa.gov/DONKI/WS/get/SEP?startDate=2024-05-01&endDate=2024-05-31); [DONKI flares](https://ccmc.gsfc.nasa.gov/DONKI/WS/get/FLR?startDate=2024-05-10&endDate=2024-05-11); [DONKI storms](https://ccmc.gsfc.nasa.gov/DONKI/WS/get/GST?startDate=2024-05-01&endDate=2024-05-31)). Two properties shape how they can be used. A particle event record holds an onset time, the detecting instrument or forecasting model and links to the causing flare, with **no flux and no dose**, so it can trigger an alert and cannot supply millisieverts. Some records are model forecasts instead of detections, and CCMC labels the whole database experimental research information, naming NOAA as the official forecaster ([CCMC DONKI](https://ccmc.gsfc.nasa.gov/tools/DONKI/)). NOAA's GOES feed fills the flux gap with five-minute readings whose 10 MeV channel maps onto the solar radiation storm scale ([NOAA SWPC proton flux](https://www.spaceweather.gov/products/goes-proton-flux)). It read about 0.2 proton flux units on November 14, far below the 10-unit storm threshold, so a live demo shows a quiet Sun unless a stored event such as May 2024 is replayed. NOAA is not on the Space Apps partner list, so its feed belongs under other references.

For ISS position, SSCWeb is the NASA-operated source that passed the test. The better-known Open Notify service is HTTP-only and fails on an HTTPS page ([Treehouse report](https://teamtreehouse.com/community/astronauts-in-space-receiving-mixed-content-error-because-everything-is-https-now-and-the-endpoint-is-http-help)), and wheretheiss.at is quick to code but is not NASA data ([wheretheiss.at docs](https://wheretheiss.at/w/developer)). Position has little bearing on crew health either way.

### What works only as a saved file

Five endpoints failed from the browser: the OSDR RadLab API, the OSDR Developer API, the OSDR Biological Data API, NTRS search and JPL Horizons. The test cannot tell a cross-origin block from a server fault, and for RadLab the doubt is real, because the researchers' own calls to its API also failed through their fetch tool that day. JPL removes the doubt for Horizons by stating that its APIs are not to be embedded in websites ([JPL SSD API](https://ssd-api.jpl.nasa.gov/)). For all five, the only route into Astro is a file committed to the repository and loaded from the app's own origin, which also removes keys, rate limits and judging-day outages; GitHub Pages limits of 1 GB per site and 100 GB of bandwidth a month are far above anything this needs ([GitHub Pages limits](https://docs.github.com/en/pages/getting-started-with-github-pages/github-pages-limits)). The RadLab and Inspiration4 files additionally have to be fetched by hand in an ordinary browser tab, and nobody has yet confirmed that either download works. OSDR's Environmental Data App is in the same position: it holds cabin temperature, humidity, carbon dioxide and daily radiation dose for 21 rodent and plant payload missions, exportable by hand with no API, and it is context for payload missions, not crew data ([OSDR Environmental Data App](https://visualization.osdr.nasa.gov/eda/)).

The api.nasa.gov catalogue adds little beyond DONKI and the image library. The Earth and Mars Rover Photos APIs are archived, the legacy picture-of-the-day endpoint goes offline on December 1, 2026, and the InSight Mars weather entry dates from 2021 and should be treated as dead ([api.nasa.gov banner source](https://github.com/nasa/api-docs/blob/gh-pages/assets/html/header.html)). A registered key cannot be hidden in a static page, and the shared demo key allows 30 requests an hour and 50 a day per IP address ([api.nasa.gov authentication](https://github.com/nasa/api-docs/blob/gh-pages/assets/html/authentication.html)). None of the confirmed-live sources above needs a key at all. The catalogue's remaining entries, such as TechPort, the Moon and Mars map tiles and a third-party orbital elements service, are cosmetic or unrelated to health.

## All five hazards have NASA sources, but only radiation has a full chain

NASA's Human Research Program groups spaceflight risk under five hazards: space radiation, isolation and confinement, distance from Earth, gravity fields, and hostile or closed environments ([NASA HRP Hazards](https://www.nasa.gov/hrp/hazards/)). Every hazard can be tied to at least one NASA source that is usable by Sunday, though the sources differ sharply in kind.

| Hazard | Astro indicators and features | NASA sources usable by Sunday | Partner and other references |
|---|---|---|---|
| Space radiation | Cumulative radiation dose | Career and solar-event limits (NASA-STD-3001 Vol 1); ISS dose rate (NTRS 20250006168); DONKI events (live); Artemis I and Mars cruise rates (published); RadLab dose rates (saved file, unconfirmed) | CSA Radi-N2 neutron dose; ESA DOSIS dose rates; JAXA PADLES (cite only); NOAA GOES protons |
| Isolation and confinement | Sleep duration; mood; wellbeing check-in | Sleep brief (8.5 hours recommended, 6 to 6.5 measured); behavioural health brief; pharmaceutical brief (sleep aids used by 71% of crew); Standard Measures panel | CSA Space Health wellness questionnaire; CSA PRET cognitive test data (ground participants); ISRO Mission MITRA (context) |
| Distance from Earth | Medication tracking; flight-surgeon report and its delay | ISS medical kit list (April 2015); pharmaceutical brief (190 medications, potency after 550 days); JPL planetary elements for signal delay; NASA's 4 to 24 minute Mars delay | CSA's list of deep-space mission risks |
| Gravity fields | Resting heart rate; blood pressure; aerobic capacity; body mass; urine volume; urinary calcium; vitamin D | Aerobic, muscle and bone requirements (Vol 1); aerobic time series (NTRS 20140003748); renal stone evidence report; nutrition book; bone loss brief | CSA Vascular (arterial stiffness, insulin resistance) |
| Hostile/closed environments | Body temperature; fasting glucose and blood potassium (via the food system); a cabin panel if built | ISS live telemetry; carbon dioxide, temperature, humidity, pressure, noise and water limits (Vol 2 through the OCHMO briefs) | CSA Bio-Monitor (design reference) |

Radiation is the one hazard where a standard, measured environment data and a live event feed all exist. The limit side is settled at 600 mSv of career effective dose for all ages and sexes and 250 mSv for the design solar particle event. On the rate side, a NASA-authored paper puts ISS effective dose at **0.2 to 0.5 mSv/day, about 40 to 90 mSv per six-month mission** ([NTRS 20250006168](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20250006168/downloads/ASCEND%20Simonsen%20draft%201.pdf)), so a six-month stay uses roughly 7 to 15 percent of the career limit. News coverage citing a 2017 NASA report gives 80 to 160 mSv for the same stay ([ABC7 News](https://abc7news.com/15270382)), and ESA's instruments in Columbus peaked at 647 µSv/day in May 2016 ([Berger et al. 2017](https://www.swsc-journal.org/articles/swsc/abs/2017/01/swsc160046/swsc160046.html)). The ESA figure is a dose equivalent reading at one location, a different quantity from the effective dose the limit is written in, and the news figure's basis is not stated. That makes the NASA-authored range the safer baseline, with the caveat that the paper is a draft and one of its tables has inconsistent units. Beyond low Earth orbit, the Artemis I measurements give 0.96 to 1.24 mSv/day from galactic cosmic rays inside Orion and 26.7 to 35.4 mSv for the whole mission, and quote 1.58 mSv/day for the cruise to Mars ([George et al. 2024](https://www.osti.gov/pages/servlets/purl/2447574)); NASA's radiation brief uses 1.3 mSv/day for free space and 0.9 mSv/day on a planetary surface ([OCHMO-TB-020](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ochmo-tb-020-radiation-protection.pdf)). The Artemis paper also supplies a basis for a shelter prompt, since Orion holds a large solar particle event to at most 150 mSv and dose rates differed fourfold between its most and least shielded locations. The lunar surface figure of 1,369 µSv/day comes from China's Chang'e 4 lander as reported in the press, so it is an other reference ([Sci.News](https://www.sci.news/space/radiation-dose-rates-lunar-surface-08893.html)).

What cannot be done is to compute a real dose from a real event. DONKI has no flux, GOES flux is not dose inside a vehicle, and NASA's own dose models are closed to a weekend project: OLTARIS needs registration with administrator approval ([OLTARIS](https://oltaris.nasa.gov/)) and NAIRAS runs through a request queue ([CCMC NAIRAS](https://ccmc.gsfc.nasa.gov/news/nairas-update)). NASA's Space Radiation Analysis Group publishes explanations and no dose dataset ([SRAG](https://srag.jsc.nasa.gov/)). Any dose increment Astro shows after a solar event is a simulation and has to say so. RadLab is the measured alternative. It gathers ISS detector series at cadences from 5 minutes to a day, together with lunar orbit, deep space and Mars surface sets ([RadLab release history](https://visualization.osdr.nasa.gov/radlab/kb/Release_history)), queryable by time window ([RadLab Data API](https://visualization.osdr.nasa.gov/radlab/gui/data-api/)). One researcher read the units on that page as micrograys and microsieverts per hour and another could not find them, so the units need checking on the export itself. Its main quantity is absorbed dose, only the ALTEA instrument is documented with dose equivalent, the date coverage per instrument is unknown, and the Artemis I detector data are not in it yet. The raw Mars and lunar archives are public but arrive in planetary archive formats that would take 4 to 8 hours to parse, so the published rates are the practical substitute ([PDS MSL RAD archive](https://pds-ppi.igpp.ucla.edu/data/MSL-M-RAD-3-RDR-V1.0/AAREADME.TXT)).

Distance from Earth is where Astro's medication tracking and flight-surgeon report live, and two NASA resources give them real content. A NASA-hosted page of the ISS Medical Checklist dated April 9, 2015 lists the station's medication packs drug by drug with strengths ([NASA medical kit checklist](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2015/03/medical_kit_checklist_-_full_release.pdf)). It is the only itemised formulary found and it is 11 years old. NASA's pharmaceutical brief, revised in January 2026, adds context: 190 medications across the two ISS kits, sleep aids used by 71 percent of crew members, and eight of nine tested drugs still within label potency after 550 days in orbit ([OCHMO pharmaceutical brief](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ochmo-tb-006-pharmaceuticals.pdf)). A separate NASA project notes that kit inventory is still tracked by hand, with crew telling the flight surgeon what they used ([NTRS 20240004505](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20240004505)), which is the problem a medication log addresses. For the report delay, NASA puts one-way Mars signal time between about 4 and 24 minutes ([NASA space communications](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/tech-demonstration/space-communications-7-things-you-need-to-know)). Horizons would give exact values but cannot be called from a web page, whereas JPL's published planetary elements let a page compute the Earth to Mars distance for any date to within about 25,000 km, under a tenth of a second of signal time ([JPL approximate positions](https://ssd.jpl.nasa.gov/planets/approx_pos.html)).

A structural layer is available across all five hazards. NASA's Human System Risk Board maintains 30 risk pages, updated through 2025, that link each risk to its evidence report and to a plain-text causal diagram ([NASA Human System Risks](https://www.nasa.gov/hhp/human-system-risks/)); nine of them map directly onto Astro's indicators and features, from renal stone formation (urine volume, urinary calcium) to behavioural health (mood). The older Human Research Roadmap offers likelihood and consequence ratings per mission type, but its renal page showed ratings last approved in 2016, so the nasa.gov pages are the current source ([HRR renal risk page](https://humanresearchroadmap.nasa.gov/Risks/risk.aspx?i=81)). Neither has an API, and NTRS search failed the browser test, so a hand-built map with fixed links is the route.

### Partner agencies add citations and constants, not feeds

A repost of NASA's registration announcement names 17 Space Agency Partners for 2026, among them CSA, ESA, JAXA, ISRO and ASI. CNES, DLR and the Australian Space Agency are absent, and the official partner page could not be read, so the list rests on one secondary source ([astrobiology.com repost](https://astrobiology.com/2026/09/04/registration-is-now-open-for-the-2026-nasa-space-apps-challenge/)). CSA matters most because two of its pages sit in the challenge's own resource list. Both are explainers with no dataset: one reports a $5.5 million contract for a neutron spectrometer and puts neutrons at about 30 percent of ISS radiation ([CSA radiation article](https://www.asc-csa.gc.ca/eng/news/articles/2026/2026-02-26-monitoring-radiation-astronaut-health.asp)), and the other lists the added risks of Moon and Mars missions ([CSA science page](https://www.asc-csa.gc.ca/eng/iss/science/why-do-we-conduct-science-experiments-in-space.asp)). CSA's Radi-N2 page gives a citable neutron dose of **about 150 µSv/day** ([CSA Radi-N2](https://www.asc-csa.gc.ca/eng/sciences/radi-n2.asp)), its Vascular studies report arteries stiffening by 17 to 30 percent over six months along with insulin resistance ([CSA Vascular](https://www.asc-csa.gc.ca/eng/sciences/vascular.asp)), and Bio-Monitor and Space Health are the closest real-world analogues to Astro's design. The only health-related dataset found on CSA's open data portal is a neurocognitive test tool evaluated on ground participants, under the Open Government Licence – Canada ([CSA PRET dataset](https://data.asc-csa.gc.ca/dataset/c22fa749-b005-4956-bf68-2ac2b9e68d31)). A Radi-N2 data folder published for Space Apps 2019 exists, but its contents could not be seen ([Radi-N2 challenge description](https://donnees-data.asc-csa.gc.ca/users/OpenData_DonneesOuvertes/pub/Space%20Apps%20Challenge%202019/RADi-N2/Radi-N2_Challenge%20description_ENG.pdf)).

ESA's archive documents a metadata query endpoint that returns JSON, untested for cross-origin access, and locks all data files behind a login ([HREDA HTTP guide](https://hreda.esac.esa.int/hreda-help/httpgeneric/index.html)). Its usable contribution is the DOSIS dataset record for citation ([ESA DOSIS DOI record](https://esdcdoi.esac.esa.int/doi/html/data/hre/hreda/23a82d40-ab38-41f2-92e2-60770eae82ac.html)) and the peak dose rates in an open-access paper under CC BY 4.0 ([Berger et al. 2017](https://www.swsc-journal.org/articles/swsc/abs/2017/01/swsc160046/swsc160046.html)). JAXA's PADLES database describes area dosimetry in Kibo since 2008, keeps its results behind per-mission search forms with no download, and sits under site terms that require credit and forbid alteration ([JAXA PADLES](https://humans-in-space.jaxa.jp/spacerad/EI000.html); [JAXA site policy](http://global.jaxa.jp/policy.html)). ISRO's Mission MITRA analog study states no data release ([ISRO Mission MITRA](https://www.isro.gov.in/mission_mitra_by_ISRO.html)), and limited searching found nothing downloadable from the remaining partners. No partner source covers vitamin D, potassium, urine volume, urinary calcium, body mass or sleep.

## Ten options, ranked: the reference layer is safest, the datasets riskiest

The live 2026 guides, read in a logged-in browser on November 13 and 14, score five criteria from 1 to 5: Impact, Creativity, Validity, Relevance and Presentation. A top Relevance score requires a project that answers the challenge directly with **NASA data as a core component and not an add-on**, and Validity looks for scientific soundness, technical feasibility, completeness and ease of use ([Judging and Awards Guide](https://www.spaceappschallenge.org/resources/judging-awards-guide)). The project page asks for specifics on at least one NASA dataset or resource and has a separate field for partner data and other references ([Project Submission Guide](https://www.spaceappschallenge.org/resources/project-submission-guide/)). The public copies of both guides still serve older editions, so this wording is a paraphrase of the live pages. Astro's present use of NASA resources meets that field's minimum; the scoring question is separate, and it is where the options below differ.

Precedent neither helps nor hurts. None of the 40 global winners from 2022 to 2025 is an astronaut health monitoring app. NASA's write-ups show data and science awards going to projects built on named datasets, while concept awards have gone to crew and habitat projects described with no dataset at all ([NASA 2025 Global Winners](https://www.nasa.gov/learning-resources/stem-engagement-at-nasa/nasa-announces-2025-international-space-apps-challenge-global-winners/); [2022 Global Winners](https://www.spaceappschallenge.org/blog/2022-global-winners/)). Whether a winner fetched its data live or stored it never appears in those write-ups, and the project pages themselves could not be read.

About 32 hours of wall-clock time remained when this report was written, at 15:49 Central on Saturday. The effort figures are the researchers' estimates for one developer working in plain JavaScript with no backend, widened in option 1 to allow for tier labels and spot-checks; none was measured. Rank reflects fit to the challenge and the 13 indicators first, then hours, then the chance of failure before the deadline or during judging.

The first seven options add up to roughly 15 to 24 developer-hours, more than two people can spend alongside a demo and a project page, so the list has to be cut, and the cut is a choice between kinds of strength. Options 1, 4 and 7 form a package with nothing that can break during judging. They make NASA's numbers the logic behind every alert and fill both data fields on the project page, and their weakness is that everything NASA in the app would still be a document and not a data stream. Options 2 and 3 add the only live NASA signals available, the most direct answer to the core-component test, at the cost of depending on a quiet Sun, an orbiting relay and a contractor's server. Options 8 and 9 would give Astro a measured NASA dataset with an accession number, the pattern NASA's write-ups associate with data awards, but each could absorb hours and return nothing. Options 5 and 6 strengthen the features that are most distinctive to Astro without touching the 13 indicators. Option 8 would move near the top on fit if its first check passes.

Four checks of about ten minutes each would shrink the uncertainty before hours are committed. Opening the RadLab portal in a normal browser tab and trying one export settles option 8. Opening the OSD-569 and OSD-575 file lists to read the file names, analytes, units and access flags settles option 9. Spot-checking the threshold numbers against their PDFs protects option 1. Reading the 2026 submission guide for the demo format protects the whole entry, since the 2025 edition capped the demo at a 30-second video or seven slides and required a public project link. The 2024 edition of the judging guide also measured the deadline in the Team Owner's local time, while the 2026 home page says only local time ([2026 Space Apps home](https://www.spaceappschallenge.org/2026/)); a Dallas registration makes that Central, and it is worth confirming on the project page.

Whatever is built, the same disclosure rules apply. The 2026 guide requires simulated data to be labelled, AI-generated code and data to be acknowledged in descriptive text and metadata, and AI-generated images or video to carry a visible watermark, with AI use described in the Use of Super Intelligence (SI) field. A clean split on the project page follows from the sources. The NASA Data field takes the standards, briefs, DONKI, ISS telemetry and any OSDR accession, each with its URL, retrieval date and the feature it drives. The partner and other references field takes CSA, ESA and JAXA material, NOAA's feed, the Lightstreamer client library, the German-led temperature study and the Chang'e 4 lunar figure. A plain statement that crew readings are simulated belongs in both the page and the app. NASA asks to be acknowledged as the source, keeps its insignia, logotype and seal out of third-party branding and AI tools, and forbids any suggestion of endorsement ([NASA media guidelines](https://www.nasa.gov/nasa-brand-center/images-and-media/); [NASA Brand Center](https://nasa.gov/nasa-brand-center/)). Its guidelines also bar attributing machine-generated statements to NASA, a passage worth re-reading on the live page before the app's text is final. OSDR asks for a dataset citation and suggests a courtesy line ([OSDR FAQ](https://science.nasa.gov/reference/osdr-faq)), CSA allows non-commercial reproduction with credit and no implied endorsement ([CSA terms](https://www.asc-csa.gc.ca/eng/terms.asp)), and JAXA requires a credit line and unaltered values. Because the Inspiration4 crew are four publicly named people, showing a cohort range instead of per-person rows is the cautious treatment if that file is used.

## Conclusion

The research changes the question. No NASA or partner dataset can stand in for the simulated readings, for any team, because crew health records are closed by law at NASA and by consent rules at the partner agencies checked. The competition therefore turns on how much real work NASA's published numbers and feeds do inside the app. Astro's existing design fits that better than expected: for the six indicators where NASA sets no threshold, NASA itself relies on clinical judgment, which is the same division of labour as an app that reports to a flight surgeon and does not diagnose.

Two findings reach past the weekend's build list. Radiation is the only indicator where a NASA standard, measured environment data and a live event feed all exist, so it is the one indicator Astro can ground at every step except the dose model itself, which stays a labelled simulation. And the stale carbon dioxide channel is a lesson in itself: a monitoring tool that presents an 11-day-old reading as current is worse than one with no reading, so showing the age of every value is part of the health-monitoring function and part of the Validity case, not demo insurance. The same logic applies to thresholds, where NASA's measured in-flight norms let Astro separate expected adaptation from a real change, something Earth reference ranges cannot do. The remaining unknowns are narrow and cheap to close: whether RadLab exports from a normal browser, what the Inspiration4 files contain, and what the 2026 guide says about the demo format.

## Addendum: two follow-up checks made after the report was written

Both checks were made in an ordinary browser tab at about 16:10 US Central on 14 November 2026.

- **OSDR RadLab.** The documented query `https://visualization.osdr.nasa.gov/radlab/api/?spacecraft=ISS&instrument=DosTel&...&format=json` returned JSON when opened directly: 164 rows for a two-hour window on 1 April 2022, with columns `timestamp`, `instrument_id`, `absorbed_dose_rate`, `altitude`, `b`, `flux`, `instrument`, `l`, `latitude`, `longitude` and `spacecraft`. The service is up; the earlier failure from the app's own origin was a cross-origin block. RadLab data can be saved into the repository as a file, and cannot be fetched live by the app.
- **Inspiration4 metabolic panel.** The OSD-575 file list (`https://osdr.nasa.gov/osdr/data/osd/files/575`) shows nine files, none restricted. `LSDS-8_Comprehensive_Metabolic_Panel_CMP_TRANSFORMED.csv` opened with HTTP 200: 28 rows (four crew, seven time points each, named like `C001_serum_L-3`), with a value, a range minimum and a range maximum column for each analyte, including glucose (mg/dL), potassium (mmol/L) and calcium (mg/dL). Fasting status is not stated in the column names.

These two results replace the report's statements that the RadLab export and the Inspiration4 file contents were unconfirmed.
