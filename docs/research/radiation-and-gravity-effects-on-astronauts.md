# Radiation and weightlessness remodel astronaut bodies

Spaceflight harms health through two separate exposures: ionising radiation, which accumulates as dose and mainly raises later-life disease risk, and the loss of gravitational loading, which starts changing fluid distribution, bone, muscle, balance, eyes and immune regulation within hours to weeks. Measured dose-equivalent rates run from about 0.2–0.7 mSv per day on the International Space Station (ISS) to roughly 1.0–1.2 mSv per day inside Orion in cislunar space and 1.8 mSv per day on the cruise to Mars, against a NASA career limit of 600 mSv that most model projections for a Mars mission exceed ([Simonsen et al. 2025](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20250006168/downloads/ASCEND%20Simonsen%20draft%201.pdf); [George et al. 2024](https://www.osti.gov/pages/servlets/purl/2447574)). In weightlessness about 2 litres of fluid move toward the head, plasma volume falls roughly 10–15%, the hip and spine lose 1–1.5% of bone density per month, and about 70% of long-duration crew show at least one sign of spaceflight-associated neuro-ocular syndrome (SANS) ([NASA Cardiovascular and Vision Laboratory](https://www.nasa.gov/directorates/esdmd/hhp/cardiovascular-and-vision); [OCHMO orthostatic intolerance brief](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ochmo-tb-019-orthostatic-intolerance.pdf); [NASA bone risk page](https://www.nasa.gov/reference/risk-of-spaceflight-induced-bone-changes/); [OCHMO SANS brief](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/ochmo-mtb-001-sans.pdf)). NASA publishes numeric in-mission limits for radiation and for only three physiological domains (aerobic capacity, muscle strength and bone density); most other systems are judged against clinical norms and each crew member's own preflight baseline ([NASA-STD-3001 Vol 1 Rev C](https://standards.nasa.gov/sites/default/files/standards/NASA/C//nasa-std-3001-vol-1-rev-c-signature.pdf)). Countermeasures are partial: about 600 minutes of exercise a week still leaves 12–21% of crew more than 20% below preflight aerobic capacity, and shielding roughly halves solar-storm exposure but trims galactic cosmic ray exposure by only about 7–15% ([OCHMO exercise brief](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ochmo-tb-031-exercise-overview.pdf); [OCHMO radiation brief](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/radiation-protection-technical-brief-ochmo.pdf)). Gravity transitions concentrate the functional risk: after about six months in orbit 60–80% of astronauts cannot finish a 10-minute tilt test on landing day, and 10–15% cannot attempt functional testing at all ([Lee et al. 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/article/10.3389/fphys.2020.00784); [Wood, Clément & Reschke 2022](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20220008621/downloads/Wood-g%20transition%20MS-%207Jun22.pdf)). The evidence is strongest for six-month stays in low Earth orbit: human experience of lunar gravity stops at 12 Apollo moonwalkers and about three days, artificial gravity has been tested in people only during bed rest, and combined radiation-plus-unloading effects are known only from rodents and cell cultures. Throughout this report measured flight data, ground analogs, animal studies and model projections are labelled as such, and disagreements between sources are stated, not resolved.

## Deep space roughly doubles or triples the ISS radiation dose rate

### Three sources respond very differently to shielding

Astronauts meet three radiation fields. Galactic cosmic rays (GCR) are a continuous, omnidirectional flux of penetrating protons and heavy nuclei that weakens at solar maximum, and typical shielding lowers GCR exposure by **only about 7%** on average. Solar particle events (SPEs) are infrequent bursts of low-to-medium-energy protons, most common near solar maximum, which aluminium or polyethylene shielding cuts by **roughly 50%**. Trapped protons and electrons are met in the South Atlantic Anomaly on ISS orbits and in the Van Allen belts on lunar trajectories; NASA's medical office treats them mainly as a long-term risk needing no shielding beyond what SPEs already require ([OCHMO radiation brief](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/radiation-protection-technical-brief-ochmo.pdf)). A 2025 NASA paper adds, as model results, that solar maximum roughly halves GCR exposure relative to solar minimum while optimised vehicle design removes only about 15% of GCR dose in free space and about 10% on a planetary surface ([Simonsen et al. 2025](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20250006168/downloads/ASCEND%20Simonsen%20draft%201.pdf)).

The fields differ in biological effect as well as penetration. GCR contains high-energy, high-mass nuclei alongside protons, and a NASA Johnson Space Center overview calls the health consequences of that mixture largely unknown ([Huff 2014](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20140005866)). Sensors inside Orion on Artemis I measured mean quality factors of 2.30, 2.63 and 3.06 at three positions, lower behind thicker shielding, so one milligray of absorbed dose in that field carries roughly two to three millisieverts of dose equivalent. On the same flight an ~80% increase in shielding mass (25 to 45 g/cm² median areal density) bought only ~30% less GCR dose equivalent, whereas shielding strongly reduced proton-belt dose ([George et al. 2024](https://www.osti.gov/pages/servlets/purl/2447574)). Secondary neutrons, created when primary particles strike the vehicle and the body, make up about 30% of what ISS crews receive ([CSA 2026](https://www.asc-csa.gc.ca/eng/news/articles/2026/2026-02-26-monitoring-radiation-astronaut-health.asp)); Canadian bubble detectors measured about **150 µSv per day** from neutrons alone between 2012 and 2020 ([CSA Radi-N2](https://www.asc-csa.gc.ca/eng/sciences/radi-n2.asp)). Rodent experiments report learning and behavioural deficits after under 50 cGy of heavy ions that equal or larger doses of gamma or X-rays did not produce, and Apollo 11–17 crews reported light flashes from single heavy nuclei crossing the retina; both statements come from a Wikipedia article built on NASA and NCRP documents ([Wikipedia, CNS effects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_nervous_system_effects_from_radiation_exposure_during_spaceflight)).

Where dose lands in the body depends on the source. Trapped-belt protons on the ISS load organs near the body surface more than deep organs, while GCR near the Moon gives a nearly uniform skin-to-core dose. That lunar result comes from the Artemis I phantom experiment, was seen only through a November 2026 news summary that called it preliminary, and came with no absolute organ doses ([Nature World News 2026](https://www.natureworldnews.com/articles/73722/20261104/radiation-near-moon-spreads-evenly-through-body-unlike-patchy-space-station-doses-artemis-i.htm)). For an SPE met with minimal protection, as on a spacewalk, modelling puts skin dose up to ten times internal-organ dose with more than five-fold variation over the body ([NASA acute radiation evidence report](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20160003870/downloads/20160003870.pdf)). The units encode these distinctions: absorbed dose is in gray (Gy), dose equivalent and effective dose in sievert (Sv), and NASA's short-term organ limits are in gray-equivalent (Gy-Eq) using tissue-specific relative biological effectiveness, except for the central nervous system (CNS), whose limits stay in plain mGy because that factor is largely unknown ([Huff, Patel & Simonsen 2017](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20170001372/downloads/20170001372.pdf)). NASA's radiation group applied a factor of 1.5 to protons in Artemis organ-dose calculations ([Whitman 2023](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20230004380/downloads/BIRA2023_Whitman_v3.pdf)). No primary NASA source for the percentage composition of GCR was retrieved, so none is quoted here.

For the lunar surface, the Chang'E 4 figure of 1.369 mSv/day (about 60 µSv per hour, recorded 3–12 January and 31 January–10 February 2019 ([Science News 2020](https://www.sciencenews.org/article/china-lunar-lander-moon-radiation-health-risks-astronauts))) disagrees with the 0.81 mSv/day in the Artemis I comparison table and with NASA's 0.5 mSv/day worked-example value. For Mars transit, the original 1.8 mSv/day (1.84 in a second account ([Space.com 2013](https://www.space.com/23875-mars-radiation-life-manned-mission.html))) disagrees with the 1.58 mSv/day in the Artemis I table. The Artemis I authors may have rescaled earlier missions to common conditions; this was not verified. Two smaller discrepancies also stand: solar particles supplied about 5% of the cruise dose according to SwRI and about 3% according to NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory ([NASA JPL 2013](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/data-from-nasa-rovers-voyage-to-mars-aids-planning/)), and the 2025 NASA paper's free-space GCR figures were extracted with the unit "mSv/yr" (0.6 at solar maximum, 1.3 at solar minimum), which contradicts every other source and almost certainly means mSv per day, but was not confirmed against the PDF ([Simonsen et al. 2025](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20250006168/downloads/ASCEND%20Simonsen%20draft%201.pdf)). The Chang'E 4 absorbed dose rate and quality factor could not be confirmed because the journal page did not load.

Every daily rate belongs to its date, because solar-cycle phase changes GCR by about a factor of two: the Mars cruise was measured in 2011–2012, Chang'E 4 in 2019 near solar minimum, Artemis I in late 2022 with solar activity rising. One model puts free-space GCR effective dose at about 2.01 mSv/day at solar minimum against 0.615 mSv/day at solar maximum ([Houri et al. 2026](https://elib.dlr.de/226165/1/ME-SBA-2026-Houri-Berger-sciadv.adz1892.pdf)). Position matters too: flying close to the Moon cut Orion's GCR dose rate by about one-third ([George et al. 2024](https://www.osti.gov/pages/servlets/purl/2447574)).

Artemis I gives the most detailed measured picture of a lunar flyby. The largest whole-mission absorbed dose was 13.47 mGy at the least-shielded sensor, belt passes supplied up to 23% of cumulative dose at one ESA dosimeter, and peak inner-belt dose rates spread four-fold from 69 µGy/min (most shielded) to 287 µGy/min (least shielded), with the storm-shelter sensor at 134 µGy/min. Inside the female phantom Helga, inner-belt doses were 1.96 mGy at the front skin, 1.00–1.19 mGy in the lungs, 0.91 mGy in the stomach, 0.95 mGy in the uterus and 0.61 mGy at the spine ([George et al. 2024](https://www.osti.gov/pages/servlets/purl/2447574)). A 2026 analysis of the phantoms reports a belt-transit effective dose of 1.61 mSv unshielded and, for the GCR phase, an internal absorbed dose rate of 0.379 mGy/day and a dose-equivalent rate of 1.18 mSv/day at a quality factor of 3.1 ([Houri et al. 2026](https://elib.dlr.de/226165/1/ME-SBA-2026-Houri-Berger-sciadv.adz1892.pdf)).

For the crewed Artemis II flight only expectations exist. Five percent of a 600 mSv limit is 30 mSv, which matches the 26.7–35.4 mSv measured on the longer Artemis I flight. A 2019 NASA analysis of the then-planned trajectory attributed about two-thirds of modelled dose to three trapped-belt passes and one-third to 12 days of GCR ([Lee, NASA SRAG 2019](https://ccmc.gsfc.nasa.gov/publicData/SWxWorkshops/2019/Lee_Rad_Ops_Exp_GSFC_2019.pdf)). NOAA reported no major solar radiation storm during the flight, although its forecasters issued more than 40 watches, warnings and alerts ([NOAA SciJinks 2026](https://scijinks.gov/news/artemis-ii-returns-how-noaa-helped-protect-astronauts-throughout-10-day-mission)). Post-flight reporting gave no crew dose in mSv and said full evaluations would take weeks, with peer-reviewed biology results expected in 6–18 months ([Fierce Sensors 2026](https://www.fiercesensors.com/sensors/artemis-crew-back-home-nasa-studies-radiation-future-flights)); none had been located by mid-November 2026.

For Mars, the cruise rate was likened by JPL to a whole-body CT scan every five or six days ([NASA JPL 2013](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/data-from-nasa-rovers-voyage-to-mars-aids-planning/)), and the surface rate is about one-third of the cruise rate ([Space.com 2013](https://www.space.com/23875-mars-radiation-life-manned-mission.html)). The National Academies concluded a Mars mission could exceed the 600 mSv standard by up to 150% ([National Academies 2021](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/26155/chapter/2)). Against that, the Artemis I authors extrapolate Mars-mission dose equivalents about 30% below earlier literature values and potentially within 600 mSv, with interplanetary GCR rates as much as 60% below previous observations ([George et al. 2024](https://www.osti.gov/pages/servlets/purl/2447574)). For scale, the most-exposed U.S. astronaut has accumulated about 240–290 mSv, cosmonauts average 380–410 mSv with a maximum near 470–500 mSv after more than 1,110 days in orbit, natural background on Earth is about 3 mSv per year, commercial aircrew receive 3–6 mSv per year, and the U.S. Department of Energy worker limit is 50 mSv per year ([Simonsen et al. 2025](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20250006168/downloads/ASCEND%20Simonsen%20draft%201.pdf)).

### NASA caps a career at 600 mSv against 1,000 mSv for most partners

NASA applies one career limit of **600 mSv effective dose** to every astronaut regardless of age or sex (requirement V1 4030) ([OCHMO radiation brief](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/radiation-protection-technical-brief-ochmo.pdf)). The figure corresponds to a mean 3% risk of exposure-induced death from cancer for a 35-year-old woman, the most susceptible case in NASA's model. It replaced a standard, written for low Earth orbit only, that capped that risk at 3% evaluated at the 95% confidence level and so produced limits from about 180 mSv (30-year-old woman) to about 700 mSv (60-year-old man); the change raised allowable exposure about three-fold for a 35-year-old woman and about 1.5-fold for a 55-year-old man, and exceeding the limit requires an agency waiver ([National Academies 2021](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/26155/chapter/2)). A six-month ISS expedition at 40–90 mSv uses about 7–15% of the limit, and the 1.01 Sv Mars estimate exceeds it by about 70%. NASA's 2025 Moon-to-Mars paper states that a Mars mission may need an exception to the standard with informed consent ([Simonsen et al. 2025](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20250006168/downloads/ASCEND%20Simonsen%20draft%201.pdf)).

Further requirements sit beside the career cap. Crew must stay below 250 mSv effective dose for a design-reference SPE based on the October 1989 proton spectrum (V1 4031), exposure from onboard nuclear technology is limited to 20 mSv per mission year (V1 4032), and the as-low-as-reasonably-achievable (ALARA) principle applies to all exposure (V1 4029; V2 6097) ([OCHMO radiation brief](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/radiation-protection-technical-brief-ochmo.pdf)). Orion's own design requirement is under 150 mSv per crew member for a large SPE ([Lee, NASA SRAG 2019](https://ccmc.gsfc.nasa.gov/publicData/SWxWorkshops/2019/Lee_Rad_Ops_Exp_GSFC_2019.pdf)), and a GCR design goal under consideration in 2023 was below 1.3 mSv/day in free space and 0.8 mSv/day on planetary surfaces ([Whitman 2023](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20230004380/downloads/BIRA2023_Whitman_v3.pdf)). ## Cancer sets the career limit while other radiation risks rest on thinner evidence

Cancer is the best-quantified radiation risk, yet it rests on extrapolation. NASA's Human Research Program expects exposure beyond low Earth orbit to raise the chance of cancer later in life, with outcomes depending on mission length, solar conditions, individual radiosensitivity and age at exposure, and it expects no notable risk of clinically detectable cancer during a mission ([NASA HRP cancer risk page](https://www.nasa.gov/hrp/risks/risk-of-radiation-carcinogenesis/)). The risk model is built on the Japanese atomic-bomb survivor Life Span Study and a linear no-threshold assumption; its listed uncertainties include low-LET human epidemiology, dose and dose-rate effectiveness factors, radiation quality factors and space physics ([National Research Council 2012](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/13343/chapter/3)). NASA described the earlier 3% limit as less than a 1-in-33 chance of early death, with an average life loss of about 15 years for those affected ([Huff, Patel & Simonsen 2017](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20170001372/downloads/20170001372.pdf)). Chromosome damage measured in 23 ISS astronauts corresponded to roughly a **0.5% increase in lifetime cancer risk** using NCRP coefficients ([George & Cucinotta 2011](https://three.jsc.nasa.gov:443/articles/BiologicalDosimetry.pdf)). No quantitative astronaut cancer epidemiology was found.

Acute radiation syndrome is firmly established, but from medical patients and accident victims exposed to acute, high-dose, low-LET radiation, and in space it matters only for a large SPE behind thin shielding. Whole-body acute doses of 0.5–1 Gy bring mild prodromal effects (nausea, vomiting, anorexia, fatigue) in a few people; 1–2 Gy bring marked prodromal and blood-forming injury with about 5% lethality near 2 Gy; about 3.25 Gy kills half of those exposed within 60 days without medical care. Dose rate matters: a few tens of mGy per hour probably cannot cause the syndrome, while about 100 mGy per hour probably causes significant vomiting within a day. Skin erythema appears in 10% of people at about 4 Gy and moist desquamation at about 14 Gy, thresholds that protracted exposure raises about three-fold. Most SPEs of the past 50 years delivered under 10 mGy, large events would deliver about 10–500 mGy at average shielding, and 370 SPEs were recorded across solar cycles 19–23. A model of the August 1972 event gives blood-forming-organ doses (female/male) of 1.43/1.01 Gy behind 0.4 g/cm², 0.39/0.29 Gy behind 5 g/cm², 0.14/0.11 Gy behind 10 g/cm² and 0.03 Gy behind 20 g/cm², with skin dose falling from about 28 Gy to 0.11 Gy over the same range ([NASA acute radiation evidence report](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20160003870/downloads/20160003870.pdf)). Symptoms appear about 4–48 hours after sub-lethal exposure ([Huff, Patel & Simonsen 2017](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20170001372/downloads/20170001372.pdf)). Career-scale ISS doses of tens to a few hundred mSv sit well below these thresholds.

Cataract is the one late effect with astronaut data. Records for 295 astronauts showed more cataracts in those with higher lens doses (average 45 mSv versus about 8 mSv) and more after high-inclination and lunar flights. In the NASA Study of Cataract in Astronauts (224 astronauts, 200 controls), posterior subcapsular cataract area was larger in more-exposed astronauts (P = 0.016), cortical opacity progressed 0.25 %-area per year per Sv (P = 0.062, borderline), and visual acuity was unaffected over about five years. A Mars mission could deliver lens doses up to 40 times that cohort's average ([NASA degenerative-risk evidence report](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20150016006/downloads/20150016006.pdf)).

Cardiovascular disease is established at high dose and uncertain at mission doses. Circulatory disease after acute doses of 0.5 Gy or more is regarded as clearly established, the risk at low dose and low dose rate is largely unknown, and no human data exist for high-LET radiation; the International Commission on Radiological Protection lowered its cited threshold for cataracts and cardiovascular effects from 2 Gy to 0.5 Gy in 2012. Atomic-bomb survivors show an excess relative risk of 0.17 per Sv for heart-disease mortality (95% CI 0.08–0.26) and 0.12 per Sv for stroke, and radiotherapy patients (about 5–50 Gy) show ischaemic heart disease risk rising about 7.4% per Gy. U.S. astronauts, by contrast, had lower circulatory mortality than the general population (standardised mortality ratio 0.33, CI 0.14–0.80), attributed to a healthy-worker effect. Adding circulatory risk to cancer risk raised overall modelled risk by about 40%, yet the same report says no accepted model for projecting degenerative risk exists ([NASA degenerative-risk evidence report](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20150016006/downloads/20150016006.pdf)). A pooled analysis found an excess relative risk for cerebrovascular disease of 0.24 per Gy (95% CI 0.06–0.41) with conflicting individual cohorts ([Miller et al. 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fphys.2022.1008640/full)). Rodent studies show endothelial dysfunction, vascular inflammation and atherosclerosis ([Simonsen et al. 2025](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20250006168/downloads/ASCEND%20Simonsen%20draft%201.pdf)).

CNS effects are suspected almost entirely on animal evidence. NASA's 2025 summary rates in-mission performance impairment from chronic GCR as possible with unknown likelihood and late neurocognitive effects as possible but uncertain, and notes that a signal linking low-dose occupational exposure to Parkinson's disease awaits confirmation ([Simonsen et al. 2025](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20250006168/downloads/ASCEND%20Simonsen%20draft%201.pdf)). In rodents, low-dose heavy ions reduced hippocampal neurogenesis and cognition, low doses of oxygen-16 and titanium-48 removed about 30% of dendrite length and branching, and 30 cGy of a mixed-ion GCR simulation impaired learning and memory. Human evidence is mixed: Chernobyl clean-up workers above 100 mSv showed more cognitive deficits, while atomic-bomb survivor studies found no radiation-related rise in dementia mortality ([Miller et al. 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fphys.2022.1008640/full)). Most animal studies used fewer than 10 animals per dose group, results vary by species and strain, dose-protraction effects are unstudied, and human epidemiology cannot currently support CNS risk estimates for space radiation ([Wikipedia, CNS effects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_nervous_system_effects_from_radiation_exposure_during_spaceflight)). NASA's stand-alone CNS evidence report was not opened.

Immune and reproductive effects at mission doses are poorly quantified. Lymphocytes are the most radiosensitive circulating blood cells and drop within hours of severe exposure, and modelled granulocytes fall to about 75% of normal after an SPE peak. Temporary male sterility may follow more than 0.5 Gy to the testes during an SPE, while ovarian doses are estimated to stay under 0.3 Gy for most shielding configurations and historical events ([NASA acute radiation evidence report](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20160003870/downloads/20160003870.pdf)). Atomic-bomb survivors show altered immune function, and irradiated rhesus monkeys (0.2–1.13 Gy) developed endometriosis in over 50% of cases versus about 25% of controls ([NASA degenerative-risk evidence report](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20150016006/downloads/20150016006.pdf)). A 2025 clinical review found human data on female reproductive health in space lacking, with mouse and in-vitro work suggesting follicle damage and premature ovarian failure ([Marin et al. 2025](https://mdpi.com/2077-0383/14/17/5975)). No quantitative immune or fertility outcomes from astronauts were found. Ranked by directness of human evidence, the order is acute syndromes and high-dose cataract and heart disease, then cancer, then low-dose cardiovascular disease, then CNS effects, then immune and fertility effects.

## Dosimeters and proton alerts drive action, and shielding mostly stops solar storms

### Personal, cabin and biological dosimetry run in parallel

NASA's standard requires real-time space-weather monitoring of SPEs and GCR (V2 6099), crew alerting when radiation is expected to exceed acceptable levels (V2 6100), and monitoring of personal dose and dose equivalent, ambient particle fluence and ambient dose (V2 6101) ([OCHMO radiation brief](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/radiation-protection-technical-brief-ochmo.pdf)). Each crew member carries a Crew Active Dosimeter, in use on the ISS since 2020: about the size of two AA batteries, it timestamps readings, sends them by Bluetooth to station laptops for downlink to NASA's Space Radiation Analysis Group (SRAG), and displays total mission dose and recent dose rate; passive dosimeters by contrast yield only a cumulative dose after return ([NASA SRAG](https://srag.jsc.nasa.gov/SpaceRadiation/How/How.cfm)). The unit weighs about 35 g and is worn except during spacewalks ([Whitman 2023](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20230004380/downloads/BIRA2023_Whitman_v3.pdf)). All four Artemis II astronauts carried one in a pocket to deep space and back ([Mirion 2026](https://www.mirion.com/discover/knowledge-hub/articles/stories/from-ram-to-cad-the-evolution-of-astronaut-radiation-monitoring-through-artemis-ii)).

Cabin instruments add dose rate and particle type. Orion's Hybrid Electronic Radiation Assessor (HERA) uses pixel detectors that classify particles (photon, electron, proton, heavy ion), measures LET and feeds the vehicle's caution-and-warning system; Artemis II carried six HERA sensors ([NASA SRAG](https://srag.jsc.nasa.gov/SpaceRadiation/How/How.cfm); [NASA Science 2026](https://science.nasa.gov/missions/artemis/artemis-2/to-protect-artemis-ii-astronauts-nasa-experts-keep-eyes-on-sun/)). On the ISS, the Radiation Assessment Detector pairs a charged-particle telescope (LET 0.1–1000 keV/µm) with a fast-neutron detector (0.5–10 MeV) and alarms the crew when dose rate reaches a level where sheltering may be needed, while Radiation Environment Monitor units at six to seven locations are read three times a day ([NASA SRAG](https://srag.jsc.nasa.gov/SpaceRadiation/How/How.cfm); [Whitman 2023](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20230004380/downloads/BIRA2023_Whitman_v3.pdf)). Partner hardware on Artemis included ESA's mobile dosimeters and the German M-42 detectors, upgraded on Artemis II to six times the earlier resolution for separating radiation types ([DLR 2024](https://www.dlr.de/en/latest/news/2024/orion-spacecraft-radiation-protection-tested-initial-findings-from-artemis-i-moon-mission); [Fierce Sensors 2026](https://www.fiercesensors.com/sensors/nasa-calls-key-radiation-protection-demo-artemis-flight)). For neutrons, each Canadian Radi-N2 session ran eight bubble detectors for seven days (six around the station, one worn by an astronaut, one in sleeping quarters), and the experiment found only minor differences among the seven U.S.-segment modules and typically lower doses in sleeping quarters ([CSA Radi-N2](https://www.asc-csa.gc.ca/eng/sciences/radi-n2.asp)); in February 2026 the agency funded continued development of a spectrometer that measures neutrons continuously and autonomously ([CSA 2026](https://www.asc-csa.gc.ca/eng/news/articles/2026/2026-02-26-monitoring-radiation-astronaut-health.asp)).

Biology supplies a third record. NASA runs chromosome-painting biodosimetry for U.S. astronauts on long ISS missions, with preflight blood giving a baseline and personal calibration curve and post-flight blood analysed within about a month of return. Post-flight aberration frequencies exceeded preflight values in every ISS crew member studied on missions of three months or more, flights of three weeks or less showed no significant rise, and biological estimates for 23 astronauts generally agreed with physical dosimetry apart from some individual outliers; translocations are preferred for long missions because dicentrics decay over months, and translocation baselines rise with age ([George & Cucinotta 2011](https://three.jsc.nasa.gov:443/articles/BiologicalDosimetry.pdf)). Artemis II also flew tissue chips holding bone-marrow tissue grown from each astronaut's own stem cells with matched ground controls ([Fierce Sensors 2026](https://www.fiercesensors.com/sensors/artemis-crew-back-home-nasa-studies-radiation-future-flights)).

### Proton-flux alerts and two onboard thresholds trigger sheltering

Action is tiered between external space-weather thresholds and internal dose-rate thresholds. NOAA declares an SPE when the flux of protons above 10 MeV exceeds 10 particles cm⁻² s⁻¹ sr⁻¹ for more than 15 minutes, which recalls SRAG to Mission Control; an "energetic" event, with protons above 100 MeV exceeding 1 particle cm⁻² s⁻¹ sr⁻¹, keeps SRAG on console. The same 2000 report cites an accumulated free-space fluence of 10⁸ protons/cm² above 10 MeV, roughly 0.6 Gy tissue dose depending on spectrum, as a significant-dose criterion ([National Research Council 2000](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/9725/chapter/4)). During Artemis II, particles at or above roughly 100 MeV prompted SRAG to assess whether protective action was needed ([NOAA SciJinks 2026](https://scijinks.gov/news/artemis-ii-returns-how-noaa-helped-protect-astronauts-throughout-10-day-mission)). SRAG officers staff Mission Control about four hours each weekday for nominal low-Earth-orbit operations and around the clock for missions beyond it ([NASA SRAG](https://srag.jsc.nasa.gov/SpaceRadiation/How/How.cfm)).

Inside Orion, a lower threshold produces a caution that prompts closer monitoring and coordination with medical and flight teams, and a higher one produces a shelter recommendation with on-screen warnings and an audible alarm; **NASA's article gives no numeric values** ([NASA Science 2026](https://science.nasa.gov/missions/artemis/artemis-2/to-protect-artemis-ii-astronauts-nasa-experts-keep-eyes-on-sun/)), and no public set-point for the ISS detector alarm or current ISS flight-rule dose action level was found. With ground contact the radiation console decides whether to shelter; without it the crew shelters on the HERA alarm ([Lee, NASA SRAG 2019](https://ccmc.gsfc.nasa.gov/publicData/SWxWorkshops/2019/Lee_Rad_Ops_Exp_GSFC_2019.pdf)). Through the flight surgeon, SRAG can recommend avoiding thinly shielded areas, moving to better-shielded ones, building a shelter, and delaying or ending a spacewalk ([NASA SRAG](https://srag.jsc.nasa.gov/SpaceRadiation/How/How.cfm)). Artemis crews can build a shelter in about 30 minutes, astronauts can respond to an event within 30–60 minutes, and lunar surface crews must stay within a one-hour radius of the lander; a NASA tool matches HERA data against 65 historical events to estimate blood-forming-organ dose and project symptoms over the first 48 hours and out to six weeks ([Whitman 2023](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20230004380/downloads/BIRA2023_Whitman_v3.pdf)). Real-time alert dosimetry is rated highly protective if a spacewalk ends within two hours, and about 95% of SPEs call only for ALARA measures, not sheltering ([OCHMO radiation brief](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/radiation-protection-technical-brief-ochmo.pdf)).

### Shelters, vests and orientation cut proton dose, and nothing validated blunts cosmic rays

Physical and operational measures are the only countermeasures in routine use. NASA guidance treats 5–20 g/cm² of aluminium or polyethylene as effective on the lunar surface, recommends at least 20 g/cm² of vehicle shielding for missions beyond six months, and sizes a storm shelter at about 10 cm water-equivalent for a once-a-century SPE and about 20 cm for a once-a-millennium event ([OCHMO radiation brief](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/radiation-protection-technical-brief-ochmo.pdf)). Modelling of Orion for a 1972-type event gives about 208–252 mSv per seated crew member against about 60–72 mSv with optimised pre-launch stowage ([Lee, NASA SRAG 2019](https://ccmc.gsfc.nasa.gov/publicData/SWxWorkshops/2019/Lee_Rad_Ops_Exp_GSFC_2019.pdf)). Artemis I data, applied to a simulated October 1989 event, show 414 µGy/min at the cabin sensor against 95 µGy/min in the shelter, about a four-fold difference ([George et al. 2024](https://www.osti.gov/pages/servlets/purl/2447574)), and reaching a 5–10 g/cm² location in time makes blood-forming-organ doses above 1 Gy highly unlikely ([NASA acute radiation evidence report](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20160003870/downloads/20160003870.pdf)). Orion's shelter is made by moving stowage against the least-shielded wall ([Space.com 2026](https://www.space.com/space-exploration/artemis/how-the-artemis-2-astronauts-could-weather-a-solar-storm-during-their-moon-mission)); on Artemis II the planned full shelter-building demonstration was cut back to a ventilation test ([Fierce Sensors 2026](https://www.fiercesensors.com/sensors/nasa-calls-key-radiation-protection-demo-artemis-flight)).

Two measured results from Artemis I extend the list. Turning the spacecraft 90° during the inner-belt pass halved the dose rate ([DLR 2024](https://www.dlr.de/en/latest/news/2024/orion-spacecraft-radiation-protection-tested-initial-findings-from-artemis-i-moon-mission)). The 26 kg polyethylene AstroRad vest cut effective dose during the belt transit by **25.8 ± 0.4%** (1.61 to 1.19 mSv), with organ reductions from 38.4% (breasts) to 2.4% (brain); the authors' flight-validated model projects 58.2% lower whole-body dose for an August 1972-type event and 36.9% lower for an October 1989-type event, but the paper reports no vest benefit for the GCR phase and no solar storm occurred on that flight ([Houri et al. 2026](https://elib.dlr.de/226165/1/ME-SBA-2026-Houri-Berger-sciadv.adz1892.pdf); [Science News 2026](https://www.sciencenews.org/article/vest-artemis-mission-blocked-radiation)).

Against GCR the levers are time and timing. Reductions above 50% are of limited feasibility, added shielding and faster transits are mass-prohibitive in the current Mars architecture, a 90-day shorter Mars transit saves about 135 mSv, and a Venus swing-by trajectory raises SPE intensity two- to more than four-fold, all as model results ([Simonsen et al. 2025](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20250006168/downloads/ASCEND%20Simonsen%20draft%201.pdf)). NASA states that **no validated medical countermeasure exists for long-term radiation effects**; products approved for acute terrestrial exposure are considered likely suitable for an unexpected large SPE ([Simonsen et al. 2025](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20250006168/downloads/ASCEND%20Simonsen%20draft%201.pdf)). Four such drugs are approved in the United States for injury from external penetrating radiation: filgrastim, pegfilgrastim, sargramostim and romiplostim ([AFRRI](https://afrri.usuhs.edu/node/64752)). Amifostine and alpha-lipoic acid protected cognition in irradiated mice and neither is validated in humans ([Miller et al. 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fphys.2022.1008640/full)). Germ-cell preservation before flight is NASA's cited route to lower reproductive risk ([NASA acute radiation evidence report](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20160003870/downloads/20160003870.pdf)). No evidence was found of any radioprotective drug or supplement flown operationally.

## Weightlessness sends 2 litres of fluid headward and unloads bone and muscle

### Plasma volume and blood pressure fall while cardiac output rises

Removing the head-to-foot hydrostatic gradient relocates about **2 litres** of fluid from the lower to the upper body on entry into weightlessness, and because crew never stand the shift lasts all day without the variation seen on Earth ([NASA Cardiovascular and Vision Laboratory](https://www.nasa.gov/directorates/esdmd/hhp/cardiovascular-and-vision)). Redistribution completes within about two days ([OCHMO orthostatic intolerance brief](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ochmo-tb-019-orthostatic-intolerance.pdf)). The visible signs are facial and periorbital swelling, clearest in the first three days, nasal congestion for hours to a few days, and headache in up to 90% of astronauts in the first few days ([OCHMO space adaptation sickness brief](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/ochmo-mtb-004-space-adaptation-sickness-sas.pdf)); an ESA page dated 2001 says each leg can lose up to about a litre within a day ([ESA](https://www.esa.int/Science_Exploration/Human_and_Robotic_Exploration/Astronauts/Living_in_space)).

The body responds by shedding plasma, and sources disagree on the amount: about 15% of intravascular volume ([OCHMO orthostatic intolerance brief](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ochmo-tb-019-orthostatic-intolerance.pdf)), about 10% ([University of Ottawa 2022](https://www.uottawa.ca/about-us/media/news/new-canadian-led-study-shows-why-astronauts-are-anemic-when-returning-space-travel)), about 20% in the 2001 ESA page, and 12.5% on Skylab with recovery by 14 days after landing ([NASA aerobic and muscle evidence report](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20220019338/downloads/Evidence%20report%20aerobic%20and%20muscle_FINAL%209-21-22%20Pubpress%20accepted_v2.pdf)). The more recent sources support 10–15%. In bed rest, six-degree head-down tilt yields 4–17% reductions ([OCHMO orthostatic intolerance brief](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ochmo-tb-019-orthostatic-intolerance.pdf)). Red cells follow: in 14 ISS astronauts on six-month missions, red-cell destruction ran about 3 million cells per second against about 2 million on the ground (54% higher), measured from carbon monoxide in exhaled breath plus blood markers; 5 of 13 sampled at landing were clinically anaemic, counts normalised three to four months after return, and destruction was still 30% above preflight at one year ([University of Ottawa 2022](https://www.uottawa.ca/about-us/media/news/new-canadian-led-study-shows-why-astronauts-are-anemic-when-returning-space-travel)).

Intracranial pressure (ICP) **has never been measured during spaceflight** ([OCHMO SANS brief](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/ochmo-mtb-001-sans.pdf)). The only direct readings come from eight non-astronaut volunteers with implanted reservoirs, studied in parabolic flight and bed rest: 4 ± 1 mmHg seated upright, 15 ± 2 mmHg supine, 13 ± 2 mmHg in acute zero gravity and 15 ± 4 mmHg after 24 hours of head-down tilt. The authors conclude that weightlessness does not raise ICP pathologically but abolishes the normal upright drop ([Lawley et al. 2017](https://researchprofiles.ku.dk/da/publications/effect-of-gravity-and-microgravity-on-intracranial-pressure/)). Post-flight lumbar punctures in astronauts with SANS, taken 12–66 days after landing, gave opening pressures of 21, 22, 28 and 28.5 cmH2O ([npj Microgravity SANS review 2020](https://link.springer.com/10.1038/s41526-020-0097-9)). NASA's SANS brief treats above 18 cmH2O as high-normal and 25 cmH2O or more as the level prompting treatment consideration or medical board review ([OCHMO SANS brief](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/ochmo-mtb-001-sans.pdf)). These sources mix units: at 1 mmHg = 1.36 cmH2O, the 13 mmHg zero-gravity reading is about 17.7 cmH2O and the post-flight 21–28.5 cmH2O values are about 15–21 mmHg.

Intraocular pressure spikes early and settles. In parabolic flight, 20–30 seconds of weightlessness lifts it about 7 mmHg. In flight, it climbed 4–7 mmHg on day 1 across six Shuttle missions and returned to seated preflight values after about four days; on long ISS missions it stays near preflight, with reported ranges of 10–16 mmHg before and 10–14 mmHg after flight ([Huang et al. 2019](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20200002256/downloads/20200002256.pdf)). No NASA numeric intraocular-pressure limit was found.

The circulation resets to a new state instead of simply deteriorating. In eight male astronauts measured between days 85 and 192 on the ISS, cardiac output was up **41 ± 9%**, stroke volume up 35 ± 10%, heart rate unchanged, systemic vascular resistance down 39 ± 4%, and 24-hour ambulatory pressure down 8 ± 2 mmHg systolic and 9 ± 2 mmHg diastolic, with the night-time dip preserved. Cardiac output was measured by rebreathing and pressure by a portable monitor every one to two hours, and the comparison baseline was seated on the ground ([Norsk et al. 2015](https://research.regionh.dk/da/publications/fluid-shifts-vasodilatation-and-ambulatory-blood-pressure-reducti/)). Structural change accumulates over months: left ventricular mass fell an average 0.74 g per week across a 340-day ISS mission despite exercise six days a week, with function preserved ([UT Southwestern 2021](https://utsouthwestern.edu/newsroom/articles/year-2021/astronauts-heart-left-smaller.html)), and arteries were 17–30% stiffer after six months in nine astronauts, who also developed insulin resistance that daily exercise did not prevent ([CSA Vascular](https://asc-csa.gc.ca/eng/sciences/vascular.asp)). In bed rest, cardiac atrophy is detectable within two weeks ([OCHMO orthostatic intolerance brief](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ochmo-tb-019-orthostatic-intolerance.pdf)). The Norsk, Trudel and MacNamara figures were read from repository abstracts and university releases, not the full papers.

NASA's standard sets no numeric in-flight value for blood pressure, heart rate or electrocardiogram parameters; requirement V1 3004 calls for the capability to record and trend vital signs and to perform cardiac, vascular and ocular imaging, laboratory analyses and electrocardiography, without frequencies ([NASA-STD-3001 section 3](https://nasa.gov/reference/3-0-health-and-medical-care)). The ISS Crew Health Care System includes electrocardiography, blood pressure cuffs, diagnostic ultrasound, blood and urine sampling kits and a urine analyzer ([JAXA](https://humans-in-space.jaxa.jp/en/biz-lab/med-in-space/healthcare/medops/system)), and Canada's Bio-Monitor shirt and headband record electrocardiogram, pulse, blood pressure, breathing rate and volume, skin temperature, blood oxygen saturation and activity ([CSA Bio-Monitor](https://asc-csa.gc.ca/eng/sciences/astroskin.asp)).

### Seven in ten long-duration crew show an eye sign, and the brain rides higher

SANS is a cluster of optic disc edema, posterior globe flattening, chorioretinal folds and hyperopic refractive shift. On long-duration flights **70% of crew have one or more signs and 18% have clinically concerning SANS**, with choroidal folds and globe flattening each in about 25% ([OCHMO SANS brief](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/ochmo-mtb-001-sans.pdf)). A 2023 NASA crew briefing gives mild-to-severe disc edema in 66%, globe flattening in 23%, chorioretinal folds in 16% (5% involving the fovea), a refractive shift of +0.75 D or more in 15% (largest single-mission shift +2.00 D), self-reported worse near vision in 57% and transient visual-field loss just after flight in 3% ([Brunstetter 2023](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20230006218/downloads/Brunstetter%20-%20Crewmember%20SANS%20Overview%20-%20Export%20Control%20-FINAL%20-%2001May23.pdf)). An earlier review put clinically significant disc edema (Frisén grade 1–3) at about 15% and found some OCT-detectable edema in nearly all astronauts tested ([npj Microgravity SANS review 2020](https://link.springer.com/10.1038/s41526-020-0097-9)). This report reads the spread between 66% and 15% as reflecting whether subclinical changes visible only on optical coherence tomography (OCT) are counted; no source states that directly.

NASA publishes specific values for this syndrome. The earliest sign of disc edema is a change in OCT total retinal thickness around the optic nerve head exceeding **19.4 µm** from preflight, which is the normal measurement variability; a post-flight lumbar puncture is indicated at Frisén grade 1 or higher (on a 0–5 scale) and/or an average peripapillary thickness rise of 55 µm or more in either eye. All crew have been correctable to 20/20 or better and no permanent vision loss has been detected, although globe flattening has persisted on MRI more than seven years after flight ([OCHMO SANS brief](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/ochmo-mtb-001-sans.pdf)). Disc edema typically resolves 3–12 months after return ([Brunstetter 2023](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20230006218/downloads/Brunstetter%20-%20Crewmember%20SANS%20Overview%20-%20Export%20Control%20-FINAL%20-%2001May23.pdf)). As an analog comparison, 30 days of head-down bed rest raised peripapillary total retinal thickness by 39 µm ([Huang et al. 2019](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20200002256/downloads/20200002256.pdf)).

The cause is unproven. Hypotheses are raised pressure in the optic nerve sheath that changes the gradient across the lamina cribrosa, venous congestion altering local perfusion, and individual anatomical or genetic susceptibility such as altered folate-dependent one-carbon metabolism; SANS cannot yet be predicted and occurs in both sexes, in novices and veterans, in one or both eyes ([Brunstetter 2023](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20230006218/downloads/Brunstetter%20-%20Crewmember%20SANS%20Overview%20-%20Export%20Control%20-FINAL%20-%2001May23.pdf)). NASA names the headward fluid shift as the leading hypothesised primary cause ([NASA Cardiovascular and Vision Laboratory](https://www.nasa.gov/directorates/esdmd/hhp/cardiovascular-and-vision)). Listed risk factors are longer missions, repeat flights, a small or crowded optic nerve head, nutritional status and local carbon dioxide pockets, against a cabin limit of 3 mmHg one-hour average partial pressure (V2 6004) ([OCHMO SANS brief](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/ochmo-mtb-001-sans.pdf)).

The brain itself moves. On post-flight MRI after ISS flights averaging 164.8 days, 17 of 18 astronauts had central sulcus narrowing against 3 of 16 after Shuttle flights averaging 13.6 days, and all 12 long-duration astronauts imaged with cine MRI showed upward brain shift ([Roberts et al. 2017](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/effects-of-spaceflight-on-astronaut-brain-structure-as-indicated-/)). Ventricular volume rose **11 ± 5.9%** after long flights against 0.04 ± 1.87% after short ones ([npj Microgravity SANS review 2020](https://link.springer.com/10.1038/s41526-020-0097-9)). In 30 astronauts, ventricular expansion grew with mission length up to about six months and then levelled off, a plateau resting on only four one-year flyers, and crew with under three years since a previous mission showed little further enlargement ([McGregor et al. 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-33331-8.pdf)).

On the ISS, eye monitoring is organised into "eye weeks" of two to three days near flight day 30, flight day 90 and 30 days before return. Fundoscopy, OCT, ocular ultrasound, visual acuity and Amsler grid are done before, during and after flight; MRI and ocular biometry only before and after; lumbar puncture only after ([OCHMO SANS brief](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/ochmo-mtb-001-sans.pdf)). The OCT unit on the station has been a Heidelberg Spectralis since 2013, upgraded after December 2018 ([npj Microgravity SANS review 2020](https://link.springer.com/10.1038/s41526-020-0097-9)). In-flight tonometry appears in a NASA table with an "ESA only, otherwise as clinically indicated" note, in text that was partly garbled on extraction ([Brunstetter 2023](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20230006218/downloads/Brunstetter%20-%20Crewmember%20SANS%20Overview%20-%20Export%20Control%20-FINAL%20-%2001May23.pdf)). Lower body negative pressure, thigh cuffs, artificial gravity and drugs remain under investigation; the fielded mitigation is optical, with each crew member carrying at least three pairs of lenses of increasing power, and acetazolamide is the only SANS-related drug stocked on the ISS ([OCHMO SANS brief](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/ochmo-mtb-001-sans.pdf)).

### Left jugular flow stalled or reversed in 6 of 11 crew

In 11 ISS crew (mean mission 210 ± 76 days), left internal jugular vein area went from 9.8 mm² seated before flight to **70.3 mm²** in flight and its pressure from 5.1 to 21.1 mmHg; flow was stagnant or retrograde in 6 of 11 around flight day 50, one occlusive thrombus was found and a second partial thrombus was identified retrospectively ([Marshall-Goebel et al. 2019](https://scholarlycommons.henryford.com/surgery_articles/390)). The in-flight vein therefore sits between the ground supine state (80.7 mm², 17.3 mmHg) and head-down tilt (107.7 mm², 20.7 mmHg). Flow was graded 1 (continuous forward) to 4 (predominantly retrograde); only the left vein was imaged, and the pressure device probably overestimates absolute pressure ([Marshall-Goebel et al. 2019, full text](https://scispace.com/pdf/assessment-of-jugular-venous-blood-flow-stasis-and-4zhb5513dy.pdf)).

The occlusive clot was found about two months into a mission on a scheduled research ultrasound and was treated on orbit with enoxaparin, then apixaban delivered by cargo vehicle, for about 90 days in total; the residual clot was gone 10 days after landing ([NEJM via EurekAlert 2020](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/824124)). NASA's 2026 guidance judges stasis the most concerning pre-thrombotic indicator, screens every crew member for deep vein thrombosis and jugular flow anomalies, and restricts spacewalks and resistance-device exercise for at least 30 days after a thrombosis. Venous ultrasound on a 180-day mission is scheduled before flight, at about 30 and 60 days after launch, 42 days before return and after landing. The brief describes spaceflight thromboses only as rare and gives no count ([OCHMO venous thrombosis brief](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/ochmo-mtb-007-venous-thrombosis-in-spaceflight.pdf)). As a countermeasure, 25 mmHg of lower body negative pressure for about an hour improved jugular flow in 10 of 17 in-flight sessions (59%) and shrank vein area from 70.3 to 44.7 mm² at flight day 50 ([Marshall-Goebel et al. 2019, full text](https://scispace.com/pdf/assessment-of-jugular-venous-blood-flow-stasis-and-4zhb5513dy.pdf)); thigh cuffs alone cut jugular cross-section by 23% ([Ebert et al.](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20160001538/downloads/20160001538.pdf)). No in-flight trial read showed that either prevents SANS.

### Hip and spine lose 1 to 1.5% of bone density a month

Reduced loading tilts bone remodelling toward resorption: osteoclast activity rises while formation is suppressed or unchanged, and calcium released from bone raises urinary calcium ([OCHMO bone loss brief](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ochmo-tb-030-bone-loss.pdf)). Average density loss is about **1–1.5% per month** over four- to six-month missions, concentrated in the weight-bearing spine and hips, and NASA notes it is not known whether the rate holds on longer missions ([NASA bone risk page](https://www.nasa.gov/reference/risk-of-spaceflight-induced-bone-changes/)). For ISS crews NASA's exercise brief quotes mean areal density changes of −2.1 to −3.7% and trabecular bone content changes of −5.3 to −8.5%, without restating site or time basis in the extract read ([OCHMO exercise brief](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ochmo-tb-031-exercise-overview.pdf)). Recovery is incomplete: in 17 astronauts scanned at 61 µm resolution, tibia failure load and density measures were still 0.9–2.1% below preflight 12 months after landing, 9 of 17 had not recovered tibia total density, and missions over six months left a median 3.9% failure-load deficit against 0.4% for shorter ones ([Gabel et al. 2022](https://doaj.org/article/66f343f830484db896f2ef767ece0c1a)). No fracture has occurred during a mission to date, and exposure beyond roughly 90–100 days is flagged as increasing bone risk ([OCHMO bone loss brief](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ochmo-tb-030-bone-loss.pdf)).

Bone has one of NASA's few numeric standards: countermeasures must keep hip and spine density at or above **95% of preflight** and the femoral neck at or above 90% (V1 4027) ([NASA-STD-3001 Vol 1 Rev C](https://standards.nasa.gov/sites/default/files/standards/NASA/C//nasa-std-3001-vol-1-rev-c-signature.pdf)). At 1–1.5% per month an unprotected six-month mission implies roughly 6–9% loss, which would breach that standard. The difficulty is that **no in-flight bone densitometry exists**: DXA scans are done before flight, within 30 days after landing and at one year, with calcium, vitamin D, parathyroid hormone and bone turnover markers followed from selection onward ([OCHMO bone loss brief](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ochmo-tb-030-bone-loss.pdf)).

Countermeasures combine loading and drugs. The Advanced Resistive Exercise Device (ARED) supplies up to 600 lbf, whereas its 300 lbf predecessor was not enough to hold preflight density. Alendronate 70 mg weekly, begun three weeks before launch and combined with ARED, protected bone better than ARED alone in an 18-astronaut study, and a 2024 study found no significant pre-to-post change with bisphosphonate plus ARED while ARED alone left trabecular deficits. Vitamin D3 maintenance is 1,000 IU daily when blood 25-OH vitamin D is 35 ng/ml or above ([OCHMO bone loss brief](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ochmo-tb-030-bone-loss.pdf)).

### Postural muscles and fitness decline despite 600 minutes of weekly exercise

Muscle loss is fastest in the groups that hold the body up against gravity. After an 8-day Shuttle flight, MRI volume losses were 10.3% in intrinsic back muscles, 8.0% in hamstrings and 6.3% in gastrocnemius plus soleus; landing-day strength in 17 Shuttle crew was down 23% in the back and 12% in the quadriceps, with chest and lats unchanged. At fibre level, a 17-day flight cut soleus slow fibres from 91% to 79% of the total, single-fibre peak force by 21% and fibre cross-section by 15%, and every Skylab crew member was in negative nitrogen balance ([NASA aerobic and muscle evidence report](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20220019338/downloads/Evidence%20report%20aerobic%20and%20muscle_FINAL%209-21-22%20Pubpress%20accepted_v2.pdf)). In six ISS crew after 117–213 days, lean lumbar paraspinal cross-section fell 19% (8,737 to 7,049 mm²) and regained about two-thirds of that by 33–67 days after landing ([Chang et al. 2016](https://escholarship.org/content/qt5v3123q0/qt5v3123q0.pdf)). Most per-muscle and fibre numbers come from Shuttle and Skylab flights of 5–84 days; the ISS-era results section of NASA's 2022 evidence report was not read, and sources differ on fibre-area loss (17%, 23% and 36% by fibre type after an 11-day flight in the evidence report, against 15% and 22% quoted in the exercise brief). With the current exercise programme a large share of ISS crew still return with losses above 10–20% in aerobic capacity, strength and endurance ([NASA aerobic and muscle evidence report](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20220019338/downloads/Evidence%20report%20aerobic%20and%20muscle_FINAL%209-21-22%20Pubpress%20accepted_v2.pdf)), and a NASA model estimates a 17.6% knee-extension strength decrement after 30 days without countermeasures, beside a second figure of 20.1% whose label was ambiguous in the extract ([OCHMO exercise brief](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ochmo-tb-031-exercise-overview.pdf)).

Aerobic capacity falls with muscle and plasma volume. On the ISS peak oxygen uptake fell about **7%** after a mean 178 days with exercise, and **12–21% of crew** dropped more than 20% in 2012–2022 data; in bed rest, non-exercising controls lost about 10% in 70 days ([OCHMO exercise brief](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ochmo-tb-031-exercise-overview.pdf)). After 9–14-day Shuttle flights peak oxygen uptake was down 22% on landing day and fully recovered within 6–9 days ([NASA aerobic and muscle evidence report](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20220019338/downloads/Evidence%20report%20aerobic%20and%20muscle_FINAL%209-21-22%20Pubpress%20accepted_v2.pdf)).

The exercise prescription is about 30 minutes of aerobic work plus 30–60 minutes of resistance training per day, and observed volume averaged about 600 minutes per week across 46 astronauts. Typical use is a treadmill at about 7 mph with harness loading averaging 68% of body mass, a cycle ergometer at an average 137 W, and ARED loads from about 122 lb (bench press) to about 239 lb for lower-body lifts. In flight, maximal oxygen uptake is measured directly by gas analysis or estimated indirectly, heart rate is tracked as a percentage of preflight maximum, guided ultrasound monitors muscle size, and a reduction of 20% or more in a fitness endpoint is treated as high risk ([OCHMO exercise brief](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ochmo-tb-031-exercise-overview.pdf)). JAXA describes a minimum of two hours of exercise per day ([JAXA](https://humans-in-space.jaxa.jp/en/biz-lab/med-in-space/healthcare/medops/system)).

### The spine lengthens and herniation risk rises 4.3-fold after return

Unloaded, the spine straightens and elongates, and standing height can grow by up to 3% ([NASA bone risk page](https://www.nasa.gov/reference/risk-of-spaceflight-induced-bone-changes/)). Torso lengthening is about 4–6 cm in a 2016 study, against 5–8 cm on ESA's 2001 page ([ESA](https://www.esa.int/Science_Exploration/Human_and_Robotic_Exploration/Astronauts/Living_in_space)). Post-flight MRI in six ISS crew found no significant or consistent change in lumbar disc height and no disc swelling one to four days after landing, contrary to the earlier swelling hypothesis; the dominant change was the 19% paraspinal lean muscle loss. Herniated disc risk is **4.3 times** that of matched aviator controls, highest in the first year and clustered in the first month after landing ([Chang et al. 2016](https://escholarship.org/content/qt5v3123q0/qt5v3123q0.pdf)). Of 728 astronauts whose records were reviewed, 382 (52%) had space adaptation back pain, 86% of it mild, 11% moderate and 3% severe, relieved most often by fetal positioning (91%), analgesics (85%) and treadmill or cycle exercise (85%) ([Kerstman et al. 2012](https://asma.kglmeridian.com/view/journals/asem/83/1/article-p2.xml)). Onset is in the first two to five days ([OCHMO space adaptation sickness brief](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/ochmo-mtb-004-space-adaptation-sickness-sas.pdf)). The MRI conclusions rest on six subjects, and no in-flight spinal imaging data were retrieved.

### Bone calcium and low urine volume raise kidney stone risk

Stone risk is a downstream consequence of bone resorption: calcium released from bone raises stone risk, and exercise and hydration are the defined mitigations, with no capability yet to treat a stone on an exploration mission ([NASA HRP renal stone page](https://www.nasa.gov/directorates/esdmd/hhp/risk-of-renal-stone-formation/)). Other contributors are the fluid shift, low water intake, high sodium and animal protein intake, and altered urinary pH. NASA's brief counts 43 stone events in 29 crew members, mostly in the post-flight career, and one in-flight episode in a cosmonaut that resolved spontaneously; the brief's "since 2016" qualifier on that count is hard to reconcile with its career-long timing table and needs checking against the PDF. Kidney stones rank second among likely causes of emergency medical evacuation in the ISS medical model ([OCHMO urinary health brief](https://www.nasa.gov/ochmo-mtb-003-urinary-health-2/)). An earlier count gave 15 symptomatic stones in 13 U.S. astronauts as of 2008; in that study crew urine volumes were under 2 L per day and calcium oxalate supersaturation above 2.0 marked higher risk ([Whitson et al.](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20090006694/downloads/20090006694.pdf)). A NASA model built on 1,517 astronaut urine chemistries found calcium and oxalate the strongest drivers of predicted stone incidence and citrate and pH the strongest protective factors, with a higher stone rate in the first year after flight ([Myers et al. 2018](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20180004680/downloads/20180004680.pdf)).

Stone-risk urine chemistry (volume, calcium, oxalate, uric acid, citrate, pH, sodium and others, with supersaturation indices) and renal ultrasound are standard before and after flight. Potassium citrate is stocked on the ISS for use at the flight surgeon's discretion and the water provision minimum is 2.5 L per crew member per day; no NASA numeric urine-output target was found ([OCHMO urinary health brief](https://www.nasa.gov/ochmo-mtb-003-urinary-health-2/)). In an in-flight trial, potassium citrate 20 mEq per day (12 ISS crew versus 18 on placebo) lowered urinary calcium excretion and kept calcium oxalate supersaturation at preflight levels ([Whitson et al.](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20090006694/downloads/20090006694.pdf)).

### About 70% of crew get motion sickness as balance cues are re-weighted

With the otolith organs no longer signalling a constant "down", gravitational unloading alters proprioception and the pattern of otolith cues during head tilt, and the adaptation that helps in orbit becomes maladaptive on return to a gravity field. Space motion sickness affects about **70% of crew**, begins within hours and usually clears in two to three days, with lower incidence and severity in repeat flyers ([Wood 2021](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20210025094/downloads/Wood-AeroMed%20UTHNeuro-Dec21.pdf)). Incidence by programme was 73% on Shuttle, 54% on Soyuz and 33% on Apollo, with over half of cases moderate to severe and some lasting more than a week ([Wood, Clément & Reschke 2022](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20220008621/downloads/Wood-g%20transition%20MS-%207Jun22.pdf)). NASA's medical brief gives 70–90% for space adaptation sickness overall and about 73% for motion sickness on a first flight; mild cases resolve in 36–48 hours, moderate within 72 hours, and severe cases can last longer. Intramuscular promethazine 25–50 mg gives up to 90% initial response, whereas pre-launch prophylaxis with meclizine, scopolamine or promethazine showed no significant benefit ([OCHMO space adaptation sickness brief](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/ochmo-mtb-004-space-adaptation-sickness-sas.pdf)). No ISS spacewalk is scheduled before 72 hours of mission elapsed time ([OCHMO sensorimotor brief](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ochmo-tb-010-sensorimotor.pdf)). Small but reliable drops in fine motor pointing, dragging and tracing occur on entering weightlessness and again on return ([Wood 2021](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20210025094/downloads/Wood-AeroMed%20UTHNeuro-Dec21.pdf)).

NASA's sensorimotor requirements (V1 4005–4010) call for preflight function within normal clinical values for age and sex and task-specific, operationally defined in-mission limits, with no numeric thresholds ([NASA-STD-3001 Vol 1 Rev C](https://standards.nasa.gov/sites/default/files/standards/NASA/C//nasa-std-3001-vol-1-rev-c-signature.pdf)). No quantitative vestibular test is described as routine in-flight medical monitoring in the sources read; testing is mainly before and after flight. Landing-day consequences are covered in the transitions section below.

### Latent herpesviruses reactivate in more than half of crew

Immune regulation is altered for the whole of a six-month mission, latent virus reactivation tracks the changes, and the clinical consequences for longer missions are unknown ([NASA HRP immune risk page](https://www.nasa.gov/directorates/esdmd/hhp/risk-of-adverse-health-event-due-to-altered-immune-response/)). At least one herpesvirus was shed by 47 of 89 Shuttle astronauts (53%) and 14 of 23 ISS astronauts (**61%**), with Epstein-Barr virus load about ten-fold higher in flight. T-cell responses to stimulation were weakened throughout flight, natural killer cell killing capacity fell and may stay impaired up to 60 days after landing, and salivary cortisol rose while DHEA fell. Only six symptomatic reactivation incidents were recorded, so shedding marks dysregulation far more often than illness. Viral shedding is measured in saliva (Epstein-Barr, varicella-zoster, herpes simplex 1) and urine (cytomegalovirus), and the authors recommend preflight vaccination where a vaccine exists ([Rooney et al. 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fmicb.2019.00016/full)). NASA's requirements (V1 4015, 4017) keep hematology and immunology values "within normative ranges" and "outside critical values" without numbers ([NASA-STD-3001 Vol 1 Rev C](https://standards.nasa.gov/sites/default/files/standards/NASA/C//nasa-std-3001-vol-1-rev-c-signature.pdf)). The sources do not isolate microgravity's share from stress, sleep disruption and radiation, and NASA's 2022 immune evidence report was not read.

The ISS medical requirements documents that fix exact frequencies for periodic health and fitness exams, blood and urine panels and body mass were not retrieved, so those cadences are not stated here.

## Human evidence on lunar and Martian gravity ends at three days

Twelve men have lived in partial gravity, for at most about three days each ([Somers et al. 2020](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20205003915/downloads/Lunar%20Transient%20Acceleration%20White%20Paper%20FINAL.pdf)). The Apollo record is thin on physiology. Heart rate averaged about 80 beats/min before descent-engine ignition and peaked near a mean of 120 beats/min just before touchdown (individual range 94–153), under a descent load that peaked above 0.4 g and ended at 0.16 g; with no blood pressure data, NASA's analysis could not separate orthostatic from psychological causes ([Pattarini et al. 2020](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/tm-20205008196.pdf)). Surface activity ran at a mean 90–100 beats/min and 234 kcal/h across 159 hours of lunar spacewalks, with suit stiffness limiting comparison to laboratory data ([Richter et al. 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fphys.2017.00583/full)). No hypotension was reported during lunar descent or ascent, but every Apollo astronaut was male ([Lee et al. 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/article/10.3389/fphys.2020.00784)). Falls on the surface are documented repeatedly, and an earlier NASA conclusion that lunar gravity is adequate for posture control rested on anecdote ([Harris et al. 2014](https://rcastoragev2.blob.core.windows.net/b9af13d8bf8459b4f32766bc9a863da3/PMC4153541.pdf)). Apollo crews had been weightless for about 4.5 days before descent; Artemis planning assumes roughly 10–11 days and an initial 6.5-day surface stay ([Pattarini et al. 2020](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/tm-20205008196.pdf)).

Human analog studies point to insufficiency without proving it. A 2017 systematic review kept 43 studies covering 0.1–0.4 g, simulated by body-weight support, tilt, parabolic flight, lower-body positive pressure or centrifugation. Lower gravity reduced ground reaction forces, heart rate, oxygen consumption and metabolic cost and gave inconsistent blood pressure results, and the reviewers judged partial gravity insufficient to maintain musculoskeletal and cardiopulmonary properties over the long term. Their caveats are severe: the longest continuous exposure was two weeks, most studies were case series, and no simulator fully reproduces true partial gravity, especially for cardiovascular responses ([Richter et al. 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fphys.2017.00583/full)). Parabolic-flight data cited by NASA suggest lunar-equivalent gravity produces a headward fluid shift similar to weightlessness, with no numbers given. After 14–16 days of head-down bed rest, subjects tilted to about 0.5 g along the body axis became presyncopal as early as 3.7 minutes and only 50% of the women completed the tilt, so orthostatic stress below 1 g can still provoke presyncope in deconditioned people ([Pattarini et al. 2020](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/tm-20205008196.pdf)). On a short-arm centrifuge, ten volunteers needed about 0.15 g before gravity influenced their perceived upright, which puts the Moon (0.17 g as stated in that paper, 0.16 g in the NASA sources above) at the functional threshold and Mars above it ([Harris et al. 2014](https://rcastoragev2.blob.core.windows.net/b9af13d8bf8459b4f32766bc9a863da3/PMC4153541.pdf)).

The best direct evidence is from mice centrifuged on the ISS, and it covers muscle only. Against ground controls, weightlessness cut soleus weight by 17% and fibre cross-section by 40%, while neither 1 g nor one-sixth g showed that atrophy; the slow-to-fast fibre shift was abolished at 1 g and only partly reversed at one-sixth g. Tissues were collected two days after landing, the lunar-gravity flights had no on-orbit 1 g control, and centrifuge radii differed between missions ([Hayashi et al. 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1038/s42003-023-04769-3)). A March 2026 paper tested 0.33 g, 0.67 g and 1 g: by its abstract, soleus cross-section was preserved at 0.33 g, the fibre transition was partly suppressed at 0.33 g and fully prevented at **0.67 g**, and 0.67 g sufficed to maintain grip strength ([Tsuji et al. 2026, publication listing](https://research.bidmc.org/mary-bouxsein/publications/033g-mitigates-muscle-atrophy-while-067g-preserves-muscle-function-and-myofiber-type)). Secondary reports give 24 mice and 28 days and note that 0.33 g is close to Martian gravity ([University of Rhode Island 2026](https://www.uri.edu/news/2026/03/do-we-have-the-muscle-for-mars/)). The full text was not opened, so group sizes and effect sizes are unverified, and no experiment at exactly 0.38 g was found. On these data neither lunar nor Martian gravity reaches the level reported as fully protective for mouse muscle.

For bone, the only dose-response data are ground rodents at 20%, 40% or 70% weight-bearing for 21–28 days, which lost bone density, structure and strength, frequently in proportion to unloading ([Swain et al. 2022](https://researchportal.northumbria.ac.uk/en/publications/bone-deconditioning-during-partial-weight-bearing-in-rodents-a-sy/)). The 2017 international artificial-gravity roadmap lists the minimum protective gravity level, the minimum daily duration and the effects of Mars gravity as open questions ([Clément 2017](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41526-017-0034-8)). No human data exist for continuous partial gravity beyond about three days in space or two weeks in analogs, and no spaceflight partial-gravity result for bone or the cardiovascular system was found.

## Landing after six months leaves most crew unable to pass a tilt test

NASA rates sensorimotor impairment risk highest during and soon after gravity transitions and names manual landings, immediate egress and early spacewalks as the most exposed operations ([NASA sensorimotor risk page](https://www.nasa.gov/directorates/esdmd/hhp/sensorimotor-risk/)). Every figure in this section is from return to Earth's 1 g; no measurement exists of human performance after moving from long weightlessness into partial gravity.

Orthostatic intolerance scales with mission length in a step. After flights of 4–18 days about 20–30% of crew become presyncopal during stand or tilt testing. On landing day 4 of 6 ISS astronauts (66%) failed a 10-minute 80-degree tilt against 13 of 66 Shuttle astronauts (20%), and 5 of 6 U.S. astronauts returning from Mir could not finish it. By three days after landing 13 of 15 ISS astronauts completed the test. Historically over 80% of women against about 20% of men became presyncopal on post-flight tilt tests, and the rates are probably underestimates because the most affected crew were sometimes untested or tested late. Hypovolaemia alone does not explain the step, because short- and long-duration crews lose similar plasma volume, and in-flight lower body negative pressure on Skylab revealed intolerance after only four to six days of flight ([NASA orthostatic intolerance evidence report](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20150007319/downloads/20150007319.pdf)). The mechanism on return is pooling of blood in the lower body while intravascular volume is still reduced, so venous return and cardiac output drop, alongside reduced baroreflex sensitivity ([OCHMO orthostatic intolerance brief](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ochmo-tb-019-orthostatic-intolerance.pdf)). A 2020 paper restates that more than 60% of astronauts returning from about six months cannot complete the tilt on landing day, with earlier work at **60–80%** ([Lee et al. 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/article/10.3389/fphys.2020.00784)). Within Shuttle flights of 2–17 days, duration did not change test survival (P = 0.72) ([Pattarini et al. 2020](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/tm-20205008196.pdf)). Presyncope in these tests means light-headedness, nausea, tunnel vision or systolic pressure below 70 mmHg ([NASA orthostatic intolerance evidence report](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20150007319/downloads/20150007319.pdf)); the field stand test is 2 minutes prone then 3.5 minutes standing, scored by the stand-minus-prone change in heart rate and mean arterial pressure, about +10 beats/min and +5 mmHg before flight in one cohort ([Lee et al. 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/article/10.3389/fphys.2020.00784)).

Motion sickness returns at landing, and here sources conflict. Shuttle re-entry nausea is given as about 15% in one NASA paper ([Wood, Clément & Reschke 2022](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20220008621/downloads/Wood-g%20transition%20MS-%207Jun22.pdf)) and re-entry motion sickness as about 30% after one- to two-week flights in a NASA briefing, which also reports up to 100% in recent field tests after six-month missions ([Wood 2021](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20210025094/downloads/Wood-AeroMed%20UTHNeuro-Dec21.pdf)). Unlike in-flight sickness, re-entry sickness does not reliably lessen with repeat flights. In 11 ISS astronauts, ratings on a 1–20 scale averaged 10 at about 1.9 hours after landing, 6 at about 12 hours and 5 at about 28 hours ([Lee et al. 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/article/10.3389/fphys.2020.00784)).

Balance and gait deficits are large and scale with duration. About one hour after landing (n = 38), 54% of ISS crew completed all functional tasks, 32% some and 15% none, percentages that sum to 101% through rounding ([Wood, Clément & Reschke 2022](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20220008621/downloads/Wood-g%20transition%20MS-%207Jun22.pdf)). A 2025 comparison of 10 short-duration and 36 ISS astronauts gives the medians below, all differences significant at p ≤ 0.002; its abstract defines long missions as 6–12 months and its methods as 5–12 months, six long-duration crew who could not complete tests were assigned the worst observed score, and no recovery follow-up was run ([Clément et al. 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2025.1677377/full)).

| Test (medians, about 4 hours after landing) | Preflight | Short flights (mean 10.4 days) | Long flights (mean 6.8 months) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sit-to-stand, time to stable stance | About 2 s | 2.62 s | 4.24 s |
| Tandem walk, eyes open, correct steps | 100% | 69.51% | 37.64% |
| Tandem walk, eyes closed, correct steps | About 80% | 32.53% | 4.17% |
| Walk-and-turn time | About 9 s | 13.51 s | 25.02 s |
| Cone turn rate | About 117–126 deg/s | 84.99 deg/s | 46.29 deg/s |

Recovery follows a consistent order. In 19 ISS crew, sit-to-stand, eyes-open tandem walk and walk-and-turn time were back to baseline by about one day, while recovery-from-fall time, eyes-closed tandem walk and turn rate were still impaired; by 6–11 days no measure differed from preflight, though eyes-closed tandem walk was still affected in about half of individuals. Two of the 19 could not do any test because of motion sickness or orthostatic intolerance ([Clément et al. 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fphys.2022.1029161/pdf)). In 33 cosmonauts tested by posturography from three days after landing, the equilibrium score in the hardest condition (eyes closed, sway-referenced surface, head tilts) fell from 48.2 to 35.9 and recovered only by day 10, against day 7 for the standard conditions; the fall-rate figures in that paper were internally inconsistent in the extracted text and should be checked against the original ([Shishkin et al. 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fncir.2023.1135434)). Moving from prone to standing took 14.8 ± 3.5 s about two hours after landing against 4.8 ± 1.7 s before flight ([Lee et al. 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/article/10.3389/fphys.2020.00784)). A day after return, eight ISS crew showed deficits in dexterity, dual-tasking, tilt perception and simulated vehicle operation that controls given 30 hours of sleep restriction did not show. NASA's field test applies posturography, tandem walk, sit-to-stand, recovery-from-fall, obstacle walk and jump-down at 1, 4, 12 and 24 hours and 7 days after landing ([Wood 2021](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20210025094/downloads/Wood-AeroMed%20UTHNeuro-Dec21.pdf)). Launch-phase effects of acceleration and vibration were not covered by the sources retrieved.

Landing itself injures. Among Soyuz landings with data (80 individual crew landings), roughly one-third of crew had a landing-related injury: 21.3% contusions or abrasions, 5.0% minor and 3.8% moderate injuries, none severe, most often at the knee, back and arm. NASA's injury model had predicted under 1% moderate and under 5% minor injuries, so it under-predicts; deconditioning is offered as a partial explanation without a quantified effect ([Newby et al.](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20210022452/downloads/HRP%20IWS%202022%20Soyuz%20Risk.pdf)). For standing crew at lunar touchdown, a 2020 NASA white paper proposes a head-to-foot dynamic-response limit of 2.7 for non-deconditioned and 2.0 for deconditioned crew, against 15.2 for seated crew, and says the standing limits rest largely on expert consensus from a very small dataset ([Somers et al. 2020](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20205003915/downloads/Lunar%20Transient%20Acceleration%20White%20Paper%20FINAL.pdf)). NASA's lunar-lander analysis limits sustained head-to-foot acceleration for standing crew to 9.81 m/s² for up to 30 seconds, falling to 1.57 m/s² at 540 seconds and beyond, and recommends orthostatic countermeasures for surface stays over 30 days ([Pattarini et al. 2020](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/tm-20205008196.pdf)).

Landing countermeasures are layered and incomplete. NASA has required end-of-mission fluid loading since 1985, yet landing-day plasma deficits of 5–19% persist, and about 30% of Shuttle astronauts were unprotected by exercise plus fluid loading when anti-g suits were not worn ([NASA orthostatic intolerance evidence report](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20150007319/downloads/20150007319.pdf)). Current guidance is 24–48 oz of salt-water solution before deorbit and gradient compression garments at about 55 mmHg at the lower leg, 30 mmHg at the thigh and 20 mmHg at the abdomen ([OCHMO orthostatic intolerance brief](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ochmo-tb-019-orthostatic-intolerance.pdf)); a flight study describes a garment at 55 mmHg at the ankle, 35 mmHg at the knee, 18 mmHg at the upper thigh and about 16 mmHg over the abdomen. Worn by 11 astronauts after 194 ± 65-day missions, that garment kept stand-test heart-rate and pressure responses close to preflight at about 2, 12 and 28 hours after landing, in a small uncontrolled sample using a test less provocative than a tilt. The same paper records a drug interaction: two of three astronauts unable to start the first stand test had received promethazine, which can promote presyncope ([Lee et al. 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/article/10.3389/fphys.2020.00784)). Post-flight reconditioning starts the day after landing and runs 45 days at two hours a day ([OCHMO exercise brief](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ochmo-tb-031-exercise-overview.pdf)). Sensorimotor countermeasures are mainly training-based and under study, with no published efficacy figures in the sources read ([NASA sensorimotor reference page](https://www.nasa.gov/reference/risk-of-altered-sensorimotor-and-vestibular-function/)).

For Mars these findings are extrapolations. NASA's evidence report notes that Mars crews will land after long flights with no ground-support team ([NASA orthostatic intolerance evidence report](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20150007319/downloads/20150007319.pdf)). Authors of the ISS functional-task study conclude that crew would need several days to regain nominal performance after a comparable weightless exposure ([Clément et al. 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fphys.2022.1029161/pdf)). In simulator landings, vestibular disruption by galvanic stimulation increased crashes and unacceptable touchdowns ([Wood 2024](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20240008075/downloads/HRP-TNO%20SM%20HLS%20brief%2028Jun24.pdf)). A NASA study comparing simulated lunar-landing performance before and after ISS missions had reported only ground data as of January 2025 ([Weiss et al.](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20240012133)).

## Daily centrifugation protected calf muscle but not fitness

The main human test of artificial gravity is a bed-rest trial, not a flight. In AGBRESA (2019), 24 adults spent 60 days in six-degree head-down bed rest in three arms of eight: control, one 30-minute centrifuge session per day, or six 5-minute sessions per day, on a 3.8 m short-arm centrifuge giving 1 g at the centre of mass and about 2 g at the feet. Aerobic capacity fell **20–24% in every arm** with no group difference (p = 0.31), and knee-extension strength fell about 29–35% in all arms (p = 0.11). Calf strength fell 35% and 31% with centrifugation against 48% in controls (p = 0.003), and jump power fell 25–26% against 33% ([AGBRESA trial paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8192329)). Time to presyncope on a test combining head-up tilt with stepped lower body negative pressure dropped by 801 ± 354 s in controls against 323 ± 235 s and 296 ± 508 s with centrifugation, so deconditioning was reduced, not prevented; the control arm began with higher baseline tolerance, which the authors list as a limitation ([Hoenemann et al. 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10286-023-00959-5)). The trial's bone, eye, cognition and sensorimotor results were not retrieved.

Earlier analog work and animal flights bracket that result. In bed-rest and dry-immersion studies of 3–28 days, intermittent centrifugation reduced orthostatic intolerance and loss of exercise capacity, did not prevent immune changes and gave inconclusive bone results. People living in a room rotating at 10 rpm for 12 days had not fully adapted. Rotation causes motion sickness, disorientation and falls through Coriolis and cross-coupled accelerations during head movement, and on a short-radius device head, heart and feet experience different gravity levels. The roadmap review gives no validated rotation-rate limit and lists the minimum effective gravity level and daily duration as open ([Clément 2017](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41526-017-0034-8)). At the other end of the dose range, continuous 1 g centrifugation of mice for about a month on the ISS eliminated soleus atrophy and the fibre-type shift ([Hayashi et al. 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1038/s42003-023-04769-3)). Nothing located tests intermediate human doses, no human has experienced sustained artificial gravity in space according to the sources read, and rotating-spacecraft designs appear only as concept references in press coverage ([Universe Today 2026](https://www.universetoday.com/articles/how-will-martian-gravity-affect-skeletal-muscle)).

## Combined radiation and unloading effects are known only from rodents and cells

No human study isolates the combined effect of radiation and altered gravity; astronaut data cannot separate the two exposures. The evidence is rodent hindlimb unloading plus acute irradiation, and cell culture.

Bone shows the most consistent direction. In mice and rats, iron ions (0.5 or 2 Gy), 1 Gy protons or 2 Gy X-rays added to unloading reduced bone strength or volume beyond unloading alone; at a low dose (25 mGy X-rays), cortical thickness fell only in the combined group while trabecular loss was no worse than with unloading alone; and under one-sixth weight-bearing, 0.17 or 0.5 Gy silicon ions lowered bone formation and raised resorption in female mice. The 2022 review classes the bone interaction as aggravated, close to additive or synergistic ([Xu, Pei & Hu 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fcell.2022.861006/pdf)). Most of these doses are acute and well above what daily mission dose rates accumulate.

Immune results depend on endpoint and time. In mice, simulated microgravity plus protons cut circulating cytotoxic T cells by about 50% and monocytes by nearly 70% four days after exposure although neither stressor alone much affected monocytes, and morbidity after bacterial challenge reached 100% in female against 60% in male mice of one strain. NASA's acute radiation evidence report likewise cites mice given radiation plus simulated weightlessness that showed fewer splenic T cells and intestinal-barrier breaks than with radiation alone, an effect not yet quantified for human dose thresholds ([NASA acute radiation evidence report](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20160003870/downloads/20160003870.pdf)). Interactions were synergistic for cytokines and cytotoxic T-cell counts, additive for myeloid counts and antagonistic for some lymphoid and granulocyte counts ([Immunity & Ageing review](https://immunityageing.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12979-024-00449-w)). Vascular results are mixed: 200 cGy gamma plus unloading did not reduce endothelium-dependent vasodilation beyond either factor alone, while 1 Gy iron ions plus unloading did. CNS results are usually antagonistic in rats, yet low-dose gamma (0.04 Gy) plus unloading produced late neurological sequelae and oxidative damage in female mice. Cell studies of chromosome damage conflict ([Xu, Pei & Hu 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fcell.2022.861006/pdf)).

The reviewers themselves call the field preliminary. Most studies used acute photon exposures, applied radiation and simulated microgravity one after the other, and relied on ground simulation; results vary with radiation quality, tissue, endpoint and sampling time ([Xu, Pei & Hu 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fcell.2022.861006/pdf)). The immunology review adds small groups (four to eight animals), strain and sex dependence, and the point that ISS shielding means many flight studies effectively test microgravity without deep-space radiation; it cites roughly 1 mSv/day for ISS crews, above the 0.2–0.7 mSv/day range in the dosimetry sources used earlier in this report ([Immunity & Ageing review](https://immunityageing.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12979-024-00449-w)). No data were located on radiation combined with Mars-level gravity or on simultaneous chronic low-dose-rate heavy-ion exposure with unloading, and no formal evidence grading of this literature was found.

## Conclusion

The two hazards run on different clocks. Radiation is a slowly accumulating total punctuated by rare, forecast-dependent events, and its main consequences arrive years after the mission; gravity effects arrive in phases with their own windows: the first 72 hours for motion sickness and fluid shift, the first months for eye, vein, bone, cardiac and ventricular change, landing day for orthostatic and balance failure, and the first year after return for disc herniation, kidney stones and incomplete bone recovery. Body systems are also linked in ways single-system limits do not capture: bone resorption drives urinary calcium and stone risk, the same fluid shift is the leading suspect behind eye, brain and jugular findings, and on landing day the standard anti-nausea drug can worsen orthostatic tolerance.

Three structural facts govern how health data from orbit can be interpreted. First, the adapted state in orbit is not the ground state: blood pressure is lower, cardiac output higher and the jugular vein about seven times its seated size, so NASA anchors most judgements to each person's preflight baseline and publishes fixed numbers for only a handful of quantities. Second, several of the variables that matter most (bone density, intracranial pressure, brain structure) cannot be measured in flight at all and are inferred from indirect indicators until return, while the onboard radiation action thresholds exist but are not public. Third, everything said about the Moon and Mars is extrapolation from low Earth orbit, short analogs and mice: the 600 mSv limit is likely exceeded on Mars by projection, the protective gravity threshold in mice (0.67 g) lies above both destinations, and today's landing countermeasures depend on ground teams that a planetary crew will not have. The first measured Artemis II crew doses and the full 2026 partial-gravity mouse results are the nearest data that would change this picture.
