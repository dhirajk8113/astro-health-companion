# Astronaut health declines on several clocks

Astronaut health does not decay along one curve: measured data show several clocks running at once, and which one applies depends on the body system. Fluids, balance and aerobic capacity shift within hours to two weeks (plasma volume falls by up to about 20% in the first day, 60 to 80% of crew get space motion sickness that clears in two to three days, and peak oxygen uptake is 17% down by flight day 15), whereas bone, eye structure and arteries change cumulatively over months, with weight-bearing bone losing 1 to 1.5% of its density per month without heavy resistance exercise and 0.3 to 0.5% with it. Immune dysregulation and a six-hour night of sleep begin around launch and persist all mission without adapting away. The sharpest penalty arrives at the return to gravity and scales with mission length: about one crew member in five cannot finish a 10-minute tilt test after flights of days, and about four in five show orthostatic hypotension after six months. Mood and cognition stay broadly stable across six-month International Space Station (ISS) missions, and clear social deterioration shows up mainly in ground isolation studies of 8 to 17 months. Past six months the measured record thins to one or two subjects per measure, and every figure for a two-to-three-year Mars mission is a model output or a risk rating. NASA's published limits are mostly set against each astronaut's own pre-flight baseline, and the outcomes with the firmest limits, bone density and muscle strength, cannot be measured in orbit.

## Five time patterns separate fast shocks from slow erosion

A crew member's risk profile on day 3 has little in common with the profile on day 150 or on landing day, so "decline with time in space" has to be read system by system. The sources behind this report, mostly NASA Human Research Program evidence reports, NASA medical technical briefs and peer-reviewed cohort studies, support five recurring time patterns. Some changes are transients that resolve within days (space motion sickness). Some are front-loaded drops followed by partial in-flight recovery (aerobic capacity). Some accumulate month on month with no demonstrated plateau (bone mineral, optic-nerve swelling, arterial wall changes). Some switch on around launch and stay on (immune dysregulation, short sleep, low plasma volume). And some are penalties paid at the return to gravity that grow with mission length (fainting on standing, loss of balance, re-entry motion sickness). Behavioral health follows none of these cleanly: six-month flight data show stability with small drifts, while longer ground simulations show later social strain.

The report keeps four kinds of evidence apart. **Measured in flight** means data collected in orbit. **Pre/post-flight** means tests before launch compared with tests after landing, which is how bone density, muscle strength and balance outcomes are known. **Ground analog** means bed rest or isolation studies on Earth, and **projection** means model output or agency risk ratings for missions that have not flown; material of these two kinds sits in paragraphs that open with those words in bold, and Mars projections are gathered in the final section. NASA standards are named as standards.

Three limits on the sourcing apply throughout. NASA-STD-3001 itself was not opened, so every standard quoted here comes from NASA technical briefs derived from it. Several primary papers were blocked to automated access, so some figures come from agency slide decks, repository abstracts, press releases or news summaries; these are flagged where they occur. And where two sources disagree, both figures are given.

## Bone thins by the month while fitness drops within two weeks

### Bone loses 0.3 to 1.5% a month, unseen by any flight instrument

Bone is the clearest case of steady accumulation. Before heavy resistance exercise was available, long-duration crews averaged **1 to 1.5% loss of areal bone mineral density (BMD) per month** at weight-bearing sites, a monthly loss NASA equates to about a year of loss in an elderly person on Earth; the figure originated with Mir cosmonauts on flights of roughly 4 to 14 months and was reported as an average across that whole range ([NASA Bone Fracture Evidence Report, 2017](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20170004597/downloads/20170004597.pdf); [NASA Osteoporosis Evidence Report, 2017](https://humanresearchroadmap.nasa.gov/evidence/reports/Osteo.pdf)). With the ISS Advanced Resistive Exercise Device (ARED) the rate dropped to **0.3 to 0.5% per month**: in a table of 11 ISS astronauts the trochanter lost 1.56% per month before the ARED and 0.5% with it, and the lumbar spine 1.06% versus 0.32% ([NASA Bone Fracture Evidence Report, 2017](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20170004597/downloads/20170004597.pdf)). Over about six months that sums to 6.0% at the total hip before the ARED and 3.7% with it ([NASA Osteoporosis Evidence Report, 2017](https://humanresearchroadmap.nasa.gov/evidence/reports/Osteo.pdf)). Density scans understate the damage to the spongy interior of bone. Quantitative CT in 14 ISS crew put trabecular loss in the hip at 2.2 to 2.7% per month, and among 46 ISS astronauts flying a mean of 178 days, BMD fell 2.1 to 3.7% depending on site while trabecular bone content fell 5.3 to 8.5%, with 15% of the cohort losing a fifth or more of femoral-neck trabecular content ([NASA Osteoporosis Evidence Report, 2017](https://humanresearchroadmap.nasa.gov/evidence/reports/Osteo.pdf); [Scott et al., 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41526-023-00256-5)). All of these are pre/post-flight comparisons.

What can be followed during flight is bone chemistry. Resorption markers climb early and stay high until shortly after landing, while formation markers stay flat or fall and rebound only about a month after return ([NASA Bone Fracture Evidence Report, 2017](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20170004597/downloads/20170004597.pdf)). Skylab crews on 28 to 84 day missions showed a rapid, sustained rise in urinary calcium, a negative calcium balance of about 5 g per month and a 100 to 150% rise in the resorption marker NTX ([NASA Osteoporosis Evidence Report, 2017](https://humanresearchroadmap.nasa.gov/evidence/reports/Osteo.pdf)). In the single subject of the 340-day Twins Study, NTX and bone-specific alkaline phosphatase (BSAP) ran 50 to 60% above baseline for the first six months and declined in the second six, a slowdown NASA attributes partly to lower exercise volume in that period ([Garrett-Bakelman et al., 2019](https://lsom.uthscsa.edu/nephrology/wp-content/uploads/sites/38/2019/04/NASA-twin-study_Science.pdf); [NASA Twins Study summary, 2019](https://www.nasa.gov/humans-in-space/nasas-twins-study-results-published-in-science-journal/)). None of the sources reviewed shows a plateau in BMD itself. NASA's 2026 medical brief flags missions beyond about 90 to 100 days as the point where bone-loss exposure grows and rates the 10-day Artemis II flight a low bone concern ([NASA OCHMO Bone Loss brief, 2026](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ochmo-tb-030-bone-loss.pdf)).

No flight system measures bone density. Blood and urine are collected before, during and after flight for NTX, BSAP, calcium, vitamin D and parathyroid hormone, and DXA bone-density scans are taken before flight, within 30 days of return, at one year and until recovery, for up to three years. NASA's standard requires countermeasures to hold hip and spine BMD at or above **95% of the pre-mission value** and the femoral neck at or above 90%; a T-score of -2.0 or better is the end-of-mission target, -1.0 or better is needed for long-duration certification, applicants below -2.5 are not selected, and Z-scores below -2.0 may warrant evaluation. The target range for serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D is 35 to 90 ng/mL ([NASA OCHMO Bone Loss brief, 2026](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ochmo-tb-030-bone-loss.pdf)). The 2017 fracture report words the boundary slightly differently, calling a T-score of -2.0 or lower a non-permissible outcome, and notes that no long-duration crew member has returned with one ([NASA Bone Fracture Evidence Report, 2017](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20170004597/downloads/20170004597.pdf)). No NASA alert threshold for in-flight bone-marker levels was found in the sources.

Beyond resistance exercise, covered below, the bone countermeasures are nutritional and pharmacological: flight menus that supply 100% of calculated energy and nutrient needs, vitamin D3 at 1,000 IU daily or 5,000 IU weekly, and caution with excess calcium, because bone loss already raises urinary calcium and with it kidney-stone risk. Alendronate 70 mg weekly, started three weeks before launch, has been flight-tested alongside ARED exercise, and NASA is considering bisphosphonates for longer flights ([NASA OCHMO Bone Loss brief, 2026](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ochmo-tb-030-bone-loss.pdf)). The 2017 osteoporosis report records an advisory recommendation that zoledronic acid be recommended for flights over six months and mandatory for a three-year Mars mission ([NASA Osteoporosis Evidence Report, 2017](https://humanresearchroadmap.nasa.gov/evidence/reports/Osteo.pdf)). Quantitative results of the alendronate flight study were not retrieved.

Recovery runs several times slower than loss. Site-specific recovery half-lives are 3 to 9 months, a 7.8% trochanter loss reached its half-recovery point at about 8.5 months, and the time to 50% recovery at the total hip was 229 days before the ARED versus 105 days with it ([NASA Osteoporosis Evidence Report, 2017](https://humanresearchroadmap.nasa.gov/evidence/reports/Osteo.pdf)). NASA's exercise brief says many crew need up to about a year ([NASA OCHMO Exercise brief, 2023](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ochmo-tb-031-exercise-overview.pdf)). Structure lags density: hip trabecular density had not recovered in 8 ISS crew scanned two to four years after return, and high-resolution scans of the tibia and radius found **9 of 17 astronauts not fully recovered at 12 months**, with longer missions and higher pre-flight bone turnover going with poorer recovery ([NASA Osteoporosis Evidence Report, 2017](https://humanresearchroadmap.nasa.gov/evidence/reports/Osteo.pdf); [ScienceDaily, 2022](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2022/07/220729173222.htm)). That last result comes from a university press release; the primary paper could not be opened.

### Leg and back muscles shrink within days while arms hold

Muscle loss starts faster than bone loss and is regional. MRI after an 8-day Shuttle mission (four crew) showed calf muscles down 3.9 to 6.3%, hamstrings 8.0% and the intrinsic back muscles 10.3%; after a 17-day flight, leg, back and gluteal volumes were 7 to 12% lower on landing day and knee-extensor force about 9 to 12% lower. Biopsies showed type I fibre area 11% smaller after 5 days and 17% smaller after 11 days, in an underpowered sample of eight. On landing day, 17 Shuttle crew had lost 23% of concentric back strength and 12% of quadriceps strength while calf strength was essentially unchanged ([NASA Muscle and Aerobic Evidence Report, 2022](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20220019338/downloads/Evidence%20report%20aerobic%20and%20muscle_FINAL%209-21-22%20Pubpress%20accepted_v2.pdf)). NASA's exercise brief quotes somewhat different Shuttle figures (quadriceps torque down 15%, hamstrings 12%, total muscle volume 4.3%) that come from different studies ([NASA OCHMO Exercise brief, 2023](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ochmo-tb-031-exercise-overview.pdf)).

After about six months with current hardware, the deficit is concentrated in the legs. In 46 ISS astronauts, lower-leg muscle cross-section and strength fell significantly while upper-body strength, total body mass, lean mass and fat mass did not change; **17% of the cohort lost a fifth or more of lower-body work capacity**, 14% of lower-body power and 11% of calf muscle size ([Scott et al., 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41526-023-00256-5)). Preliminary data on 98 ISS crew show knee strength down 14%, knee endurance 11% and ankle strength 13%, and across ISS studies the mean isokinetic decline is 6 to 17%. Calf biopsies from nine ISS crew found type I fibre cross-section down 24 to 33% and soleus type I fibre power down 45%. Early ISS crews using the iRED, the ARED's weaker predecessor with a 300 lb maximum, lost 10 to 18% of calf volume in 161 to 194 days, against 4 to 12% of leg and back volume lost in the first 8 to 17 days of Shuttle flights; that comparison suggests atrophy slows after the first weeks, although no single dataset tracks muscle size continuously ([NASA Muscle and Aerobic Evidence Report, 2022](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20220019338/downloads/Evidence%20report%20aerobic%20and%20muscle_FINAL%209-21-22%20Pubpress%20accepted_v2.pdf)). Whole-body mass is therefore a poor guide to leg-muscle status.

In flight, muscle size can be followed with remotely guided self-ultrasound (one programme logged 74 sessions over 168 days), and every exercise session records loads, speeds and session counts per device. Isokinetic dynamometry, DXA, MRI and functional fitness tests are ground-only, done about 60 days before launch and 7 and 30 days after return ([NASA OCHMO Exercise brief, 2023](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ochmo-tb-031-exercise-overview.pdf); [NASA Muscle and Aerobic Evidence Report, 2022](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20220019338/downloads/Evidence%20report%20aerobic%20and%20muscle_FINAL%209-21-22%20Pubpress%20accepted_v2.pdf)). NASA's standard is to keep in-mission strength at or above **80% of baseline**, and researchers have used a loss of 20% or more as the marker of a clinically meaningful decrement ([NASA OCHMO Exercise brief, 2023](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ochmo-tb-031-exercise-overview.pdf); [Scott et al., 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41526-023-00256-5)). Because the strength standard refers to a test that cannot be run in orbit, in-flight status rests on proxies: loads achieved on the exercise devices and ultrasound muscle size.

Most muscle function returns within about a month. Nine of 11 serially tested endpoints in the 46-astronaut cohort were at or near pre-flight about 7 days after landing, NASA's brief puts the return of force, work and power at about 30 days with reconditioning complete in 30 to 45 days, and the evidence report records residual strength deficits of 1 to 9% at one month ([Scott et al., 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41526-023-00256-5); [NASA OCHMO Exercise brief, 2023](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ochmo-tb-031-exercise-overview.pdf); [NASA Muscle and Aerobic Evidence Report, 2022](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20220019338/downloads/Evidence%20report%20aerobic%20and%20muscle_FINAL%209-21-22%20Pubpress%20accepted_v2.pdf)). For missions of 9 to 14 months no published group data on strength were found; NASA's CIPHER study is collecting bone and muscle measurements on missions from weeks to one year ([NASA bone and muscle page, 2023](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/station/iss-research/counteracting-bone-and-muscle-loss-in-microgravity/)).

**Ground analog.** Bed rest without exercise produces about a 17.6% loss of knee-extension strength at an estimated 30 days ([NASA OCHMO Exercise brief, 2023](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ochmo-tb-031-exercise-overview.pdf)).

### Aerobic capacity falls 17% by day 15, then partly rebounds

Aerobic capacity is front-loaded. On 9 and 14 day Shuttle flights, peak oxygen uptake (VO2peak) measured on flight days 5 to 8 still matched pre-flight, yet it was 22% lower on landing day ([NASA Muscle and Aerobic Evidence Report, 2022](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20220019338/downloads/Evidence%20report%20aerobic%20and%20muscle_FINAL%209-21-22%20Pubpress%20accepted_v2.pdf)). On the ISS, 14 astronauts tested by cycle ergometer were **17% below pre-flight on flight day 15** (95% CI -22 to -13%), with peak workload down 24%; after that VO2peak climbed slowly, by about 0.001 L/min per day, without regaining the pre-flight level ([Moore et al., 2014](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20140003748/downloads/20140003748.pdf); [NASA Muscle and Aerobic Evidence Report, 2022](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20220019338/downloads/Evidence%20report%20aerobic%20and%20muscle_FINAL%209-21-22%20Pubpress%20accepted_v2.pdf)). Training intensity separated the crews: four astronauts who held VO2peak within 3% of pre-flight trained at 79% of peak heart rate, against 68% for the rest ([NASA Muscle and Aerobic Evidence Report, 2022](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20220019338/downloads/Evidence%20report%20aerobic%20and%20muscle_FINAL%209-21-22%20Pubpress%20accepted_v2.pdf)). In 2012 to 2022 data, between 12 and 21% of ISS crew fell more than 20% below their pre-mission capacity at some point in flight ([NASA OCHMO Exercise brief, 2023](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ochmo-tb-031-exercise-overview.pdf)).

Landing adds a second penalty, and studies size it differently. The 14-astronaut study measured VO2peak 15% below pre-flight one day after return; the 46-astronaut cohort averaged a 7.4% loss, with 7% of crew down a fifth or more; and in 47 ISS crew, 53% lost more than 10%, 15% lost more than 20% and 30% were unchanged within measurement error ([Moore et al., 2014](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20140003748/downloads/20140003748.pdf); [Scott et al., 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41526-023-00256-5); [NASA Muscle and Aerobic Evidence Report, 2022](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20220019338/downloads/Evidence%20report%20aerobic%20and%20muscle_FINAL%209-21-22%20Pubpress%20accepted_v2.pdf)). The two main studies also disagree on recovery. The first found VO2peak within 2% of pre-flight by 30 days after return; the second found cardiorespiratory fitness and leg-press power still below pre-flight at about 30 days. After 8 to 14 day Shuttle flights, a mean 18% landing-day loss was gone by 14 days after landing ([NASA Muscle and Aerobic Evidence Report, 2022](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20220019338/downloads/Evidence%20report%20aerobic%20and%20muscle_FINAL%209-21-22%20Pubpress%20accepted_v2.pdf)).

This is one of the few capacities tested directly in orbit: a graded cycle test to peak effort on the CEVIS ergometer with gas analysis from the Portable Pulmonary Function System, run on about flight day 15 and then roughly every 30 days ([Moore et al., 2014](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20140003748/downloads/20140003748.pdf)). A test counts as maximal when four of five criteria are met, among them a respiratory exchange ratio of at least 1.10, a plateau in oxygen uptake, perceived exertion of at least 19 and a heart rate within 10 beats of the age-predicted maximum ([NASA Muscle and Aerobic Evidence Report, 2022](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20220019338/downloads/Evidence%20report%20aerobic%20and%20muscle_FINAL%209-21-22%20Pubpress%20accepted_v2.pdf)). Heart rate is logged in every exercise session against a pre-flight maximum; over 178-day missions, 69% of treadmill and 76% of cycle sessions exceeded 70% of that maximum ([NASA OCHMO Exercise brief, 2023](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ochmo-tb-031-exercise-overview.pdf)). Peak exercise heart rate itself did not differ from pre-flight at any in-flight test ([Moore et al., 2014](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20140003748/downloads/20140003748.pdf)).

NASA's standard keeps in-mission aerobic capacity at or above **80% of the pre-mission value**, with a VO2max of at least 32.9 mL/kg/min for microgravity spacewalks and 36.5 mL/kg/min for surface spacewalks ([NASA OCHMO Exercise brief, 2023](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ochmo-tb-031-exercise-overview.pdf)). The evidence report adds that even a 10% loss could severely limit tasks needing 85% of pre-flight VO2peak, and that suited emergency egress costs about 70% of VO2peak ([NASA Muscle and Aerobic Evidence Report, 2022](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20220019338/downloads/Evidence%20report%20aerobic%20and%20muscle_FINAL%209-21-22%20Pubpress%20accepted_v2.pdf)).

**Ground analog.** Bed rest data account for the early drop: plasma volume falls about 10% in 24 hours and 12% by day 3, and plasma-volume loss explains roughly 70% of the variance in VO2peak decline over the first 30 days. Without exercise, bed rest yields about a 20.1% aerobic loss at an estimated 30 days, while 70 days of bed rest with exercise preserved peak capacity against a fall of about 10% in controls ([NASA Muscle and Aerobic Evidence Report, 2022](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20220019338/downloads/Evidence%20report%20aerobic%20and%20muscle_FINAL%209-21-22%20Pubpress%20accepted_v2.pdf); [NASA OCHMO Exercise brief, 2023](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ochmo-tb-031-exercise-overview.pdf)).

### Circulation looks calm in orbit and fails the stand test at landing

The cardiovascular system changes in three phases. The first is fluid adjustment within hours: a 2022 review reports that plasma volume can fall by about **20% within 24 hours** of reaching microgravity, that central venous pressure dropped within 8 hours of launch, and that four astronauts who did no maintenance exercise lost 12% of left-ventricular mass in 10 days ([Sy et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9762974)). In-flight lower-body negative pressure (LBNP) testing exposed reduced orthostatic tolerance after only 4 to 6 days. Plasma volume does not keep falling afterwards: landing-day deficits were 4.4% on Apollo and 8.4 to 15.9% on the three Skylab missions, and short and long flights show similar losses ([NASA Orthostatic Intolerance Evidence Report, 2015](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20150007319/downloads/20150007319.pdf)).

The second phase is a stable in-flight state that looks benign on ordinary vital signs. Eight male ISS astronauts had higher cardiac output and lower blood pressure in flight than on the ground, the Twins Study subject's cardiac output averaged about 10% above pre-flight for 340 days with a moderate fall in blood pressure, and ejection fraction was unchanged after a 237-day flight ([Sy et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9762974); [Garrett-Bakelman et al., 2019](https://lsom.uthscsa.edu/nephrology/wp-content/uploads/sites/38/2019/04/NASA-twin-study_Science.pdf)). Only the direction of the blood-pressure change is sourced, because the magnitudes in the primary study could not be verified. Atrial fibrillation affects about 5% of astronauts, similar to the general population, and no ECG changes were recorded in 59 Mir crew over six months ([Sy et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9762974)).

The third phase is slow structural change across months. The Canadian Vascular study reported arteries **17 to 30% stiffer after six months**, which the Canadian Space Agency equates to 10 to 20 years of normal ageing ([Canadian Space Agency, 2025](https://www.asc-csa.gc.ca/eng/sciences/vascular.asp)). The finding has caveats: the agency and a later letter describe nine astronauts while the paper's abstract record describes eight, the stiffness was measured within 38 hours of return, and a separate NASA cohort of 13 astronauts found no statistically significant change in carotid measures during or after flight ([Hughson et al., 2016, abstract record](https://dissem.in/p/70048131/increased-postflight-carotid-artery-stiffness-and-inflight-insulin-resistance-resulting-from-6-mo-spaceflight-in-male-and-female-astronauts); [Lee et al., J Appl Physiol 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7790133)). In the one-year subject, carotid wall thickness rose in flight, did not keep increasing in months 7 to 12, and was still raised at the end of the study ([Garrett-Bakelman et al., 2019](https://lsom.uthscsa.edu/nephrology/wp-content/uploads/sites/38/2019/04/NASA-twin-study_Science.pdf); [NASA Twins Study feature, 2019](https://www.nasa.gov/feature/nasa-s-twins-study-results-published-in-science)). Veins change too. At about flight day 50, 6 of 11 ISS crew had stagnant or reversed internal-jugular flow, jugular cross-section had grown from 9.8 mm² seated before flight to 70.3 mm², and one occlusive jugular thrombus was found and treated with anticoagulants ([Marshall-Goebel et al., 2019](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2755307)).

The hazard concentrates at landing. After flights of 4 to 18 days, about 20 to 30% of crew become presyncopal (close to fainting) and 13 of 66 Shuttle crew (20%) could not finish a 10-minute tilt test on landing day; after about six months, 5 of 6 Mir crew and 4 of 6 ISS crew failed it, and about **80% show orthostatic hypotension**, figures NASA says probably understate the true rate. Since plasma loss is similar after short and long flights, the rise with duration implies cardiac, vascular and autonomic remodelling on top of low blood volume. After short flights more than 80% of women and about 20% of men became presyncopal on tilt, in a sample of 6 women and 30 men. Tolerance returns quickly: 8 of 9 ISS astronauts completed the test one day after landing and 13 of 15 by day three ([NASA Orthostatic Intolerance Evidence Report, 2015](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20150007319/downloads/20150007319.pdf)). The raised resting and submaximal heart rates seen one day after six-month missions were back to pre-flight, along with stroke volume, by day 30 ([Moore et al., 2014](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20140003748/downloads/20140003748.pdf)).

In-flight tools are ultrasound (carotid diameter and wall thickness, arterial elasticity, limb blood flow, jugular area and Doppler flow graded 1 to 4), a compression device for jugular pressure, an inflatable cuff for blood pressure, cardiac output and stroke volume measurements, and the Canadian Bio-Monitor shirt for heart rate, breathing rate and activity ([Marshall-Goebel et al., 2019](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2755307); [Canadian Space Agency, 2025](https://www.asc-csa.gc.ca/eng/sciences/vascular.asp); [Garrett-Bakelman et al., 2019](https://lsom.uthscsa.edu/nephrology/wp-content/uploads/sites/38/2019/04/NASA-twin-study_Science.pdf)). Orthostatic tolerance is tested after landing with an 80-degree head-up tilt for 10 minutes or shorter stand tests, stopped for light-headedness, nausea, tunnel vision or a systolic pressure under 70 mmHg; systolic pressure fell 31.8 mmHg in presyncopal subjects against 4.1 mmHg in those who tolerated the test ([NASA Orthostatic Intolerance Evidence Report, 2015](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20150007319/downloads/20150007319.pdf)). Jugular flow grades 3 (stagnant) and 4 (retrograde) were the abnormal findings that preceded discovery of the thrombus ([Marshall-Goebel et al., 2019](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2755307)). No NASA numeric alert thresholds for in-flight resting heart rate, blood pressure or arterial stiffness were found. Resting values move little, and in a direction that looks healthy, so it is provocation tests and imaging that reveal the change.

Landing-day protection combines fluid loading (18 to 20 mL/kg of salt water taken in the final 12 to 20 hours), which improves but does not normalise heart-rate and pressure responses, with compression garments; NASA's gradient garment prevented post-flight tachycardia and stroke-volume drop in seven Shuttle astronauts, and the Russian Kentavr is worn for up to four days. Fludrocortisone preserved plasma volume in seven crew without improving orthostatic tolerance, and midodrine prevented presyncope in one astronaut case, with a caution about QT prolongation ([NASA Orthostatic Intolerance Evidence Report, 2015](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20150007319/downloads/20150007319.pdf)). In flight, LBNP improved jugular flow in 10 of 17 sessions ([Marshall-Goebel et al., 2019](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2755307)).

**Ground analog.** Bed rest shrinks left- and right-ventricular mass by about 1% per week ([Sy et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9762974)). Fourteen days of head-down tilt cut LBNP tolerance by 24%, exercise inside an LBNP chamber limited the loss of tilt tolerance to 13% versus 34% in controls over 30 days, the gradient compression garment abolished tilt intolerance in 15 bed-rest subjects, and midodrine prevented presyncope after two weeks of bed rest ([NASA Orthostatic Intolerance Evidence Report, 2015](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20150007319/downloads/20150007319.pdf)).

### Daily exercise cuts bone loss by two thirds but leaves leg deficits

The shared countermeasure for bone, muscle, fitness and the heart is daily exercise on three ISS devices: the ARED for resistance (up to 600 lbf, about two to three times body weight), the T2 treadmill and the CEVIS cycle ([NASA OCHMO Bone Loss brief, 2026](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ochmo-tb-030-bone-loss.pdf); [Scott et al., 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41526-023-00256-5)). The prescription is 30 to 60 minutes of aerobic work five to six days a week plus 60 to 75 minutes of resistance work three to six days a week, and logged exercise averaged about 600 minutes per week per crew member ([Scott et al., 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41526-023-00256-5); [NASA OCHMO Exercise brief, 2023](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ochmo-tb-031-exercise-overview.pdf)). Sources quote different daily totals, a 2.5-hour scheduled block of which about 75 minutes is exercise, or an average of two hours a day; the research notes reconcile them by separating set-up and stowage time from time under load ([NASA Muscle and Aerobic Evidence Report, 2022](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20220019338/downloads/Evidence%20report%20aerobic%20and%20muscle_FINAL%209-21-22%20Pubpress%20accepted_v2.pdf); [NASA bone and muscle page, 2023](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/station/iss-research/counteracting-bone-and-muscle-loss-in-microgravity/)).

Its record is uneven. ARED-era exercise cut bone loss by roughly two thirds and holds whole-body lean mass, but ARED users (66 crew) showed no statistically significant strength advantage over iRED users (25 crew), and six-month crews still land with average deficits of about 7% in VO2peak, 6 to 17% in leg strength and 2 to 4% in BMD ([NASA Osteoporosis Evidence Report, 2017](https://humanresearchroadmap.nasa.gov/evidence/reports/Osteo.pdf); [NASA Muscle and Aerobic Evidence Report, 2022](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20220019338/downloads/Evidence%20report%20aerobic%20and%20muscle_FINAL%209-21-22%20Pubpress%20accepted_v2.pdf); [Scott et al., 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41526-023-00256-5)). A high-intensity, lower-volume protocol called SPRINT did no better on VO2peak than the standard prescription, at -10% versus -7% ([NASA Muscle and Aerobic Evidence Report, 2022](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20220019338/downloads/Evidence%20report%20aerobic%20and%20muscle_FINAL%209-21-22%20Pubpress%20accepted_v2.pdf)). After landing, reconditioning starts the next day and runs about two hours a day, seven days a week, for 45 days ([NASA OCHMO Exercise brief, 2023](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ochmo-tb-031-exercise-overview.pdf)). An ongoing ISS study, Zero T2, compares crew who skip the treadmill and use minimal equipment with crew on the full suite ([NASA bone and muscle page, 2023](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/station/iss-research/counteracting-bone-and-muscle-loss-in-microgravity/)).

## Eyes swell, immunity stays suppressed and sleep stays short

### SANS signs reach about 70% of long-duration crew

Spaceflight-associated neuro-ocular syndrome (SANS) is a cluster of structural changes in the eye and brain (optic disc edema, flattening of the back of the globe, folds in the choroid and retina, a far-sighted shift of more than 0.75 dioptres, and cotton wool spots) whose working definition has not been formally validated ([Lee et al., 2020](https://link.springer.com/10.1038/s41526-020-0097-9)). How common it is depends on the criterion. NASA's 2023 crew briefing reports some sign in about **70% of long-duration crew** and clinically concerning SANS in **18%**; a 2026 expert consensus counts at least one ocular finding in 52 of 64 astronauts (81%) and optic disc edema in 66% by the optical coherence tomography (OCT) threshold; a 2020 review puts clinically significant disc edema near 15% ([NASA SANS briefing, 2023](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20230006218/downloads/Brunstetter%20-%20Crewmember%20SANS%20Overview%20-%20Export%20Control%20-FINAL%20-%2001May23.pdf); [SANS expert consensus, Eye 2026](https://link.springer.com/10.1038/s41433-026-04651-6); [Lee et al., 2020](https://link.springer.com/10.1038/s41526-020-0097-9)). These figures count different things and do not contradict one another.

Duration matters, although no numeric incidence for flights under 30 days was found. MRI comparisons show an upward brain shift in 12 of 12 long-duration astronauts and none of 6 short-duration ones, and ventricular volume up 11% after long flights against essentially no change after short ones ([Lee et al., 2020](https://link.springer.com/10.1038/s41526-020-0097-9)). Optic nerve sheath changes have been documented as early as day 10 ([SANS expert consensus, Eye 2026](https://link.springer.com/10.1038/s41433-026-04651-6)). In 11 astronauts averaging 170 days, optic nerve head rim tissue was 35.7 microns thicker and the peripapillary choroid 43 microns thicker by flight day 150, with change beginning early and usually peaking near the end of the mission; these numbers come from a news summary of the paper ([AJMC, 2020](https://www.ajmc.com/view/how-does-spaceflight-change-astronauts-eyes)). Two men on a one-year mission peaked in retinal thickness at different times (+135 microns at day 90 in one, +45 microns at day 210 in the other) while rim width was still increasing at day 270, and their choroid and axial-length changes resembled the six-month group's ([Macias et al., 2021, repository record](https://scholarlycommons.henryford.com/surgery_articles/493)). In a 2024 severe case, retinal thickness rose by 212 and 232 microns by flight days 84 and 126 and then decreased in flight ([Optometry Times, 2024](https://www.optometrytimes.com/view/report-cites-potential-risk-factors-for-severe-spaceflight-associated-neuro-ocular-syndrome)).

OCT, aboard the ISS since 2013, is the primary detection tool, supported at each timepoint by retinal photography, ocular ultrasound, visual acuity, an Amsler grid and a symptom survey; visual-field testing is pre/post-flight only ([NASA SANS briefing, 2023](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20230006218/downloads/Brunstetter%20-%20Crewmember%20SANS%20Overview%20-%20Export%20Control%20-FINAL%20-%2001May23.pdf)). On six-month missions the eye exams fall on mission days 30 and 90 and 30 days before landing, with MRI before and after flight ([SANS expert consensus, Eye 2026](https://link.springer.com/10.1038/s41433-026-04651-6)). The sources differ on tonometry, which the 2020 review lists among on-orbit tools and NASA's 2023 briefing marks as "ESA only" ([Lee et al., 2020](https://link.springer.com/10.1038/s41526-020-0097-9); [NASA SANS briefing, 2023](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20230006218/downloads/Brunstetter%20-%20Crewmember%20SANS%20Overview%20-%20Export%20Control%20-FINAL%20-%2001May23.pdf)).

The earliest objective threshold is a **20 micron rise in peripapillary total retinal thickness** over the pre-flight scan. A rise of 55 microns or more is one of the criteria for a post-flight lumbar puncture, and a refractive shift of +0.75 dioptres or more and Frisen grade 1 or higher disc edema are the other flagged findings ([SANS expert consensus, Eye 2026](https://link.springer.com/10.1038/s41433-026-04651-6)). SANS is usually symptomless unless vision blurs, corrected vision has stayed 20/20 or better, and NASA reports no permanent loss of vision and no mission objective lost to it ([NASA SANS briefing, 2023](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20230006218/downloads/Brunstetter%20-%20Crewmember%20SANS%20Overview%20-%20Export%20Control%20-FINAL%20-%2001May23.pdf)). NASA's Class A to D grading table could not be extracted, so the criteria for each class are missing here.

The only established countermeasure is a set of spare "space anticipation" glasses of increasing power; LBNP, thigh cuffs, acetazolamide, B-vitamin supplements and artificial gravity are under study ([NASA SANS briefing, 2023](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20230006218/downloads/Brunstetter%20-%20Crewmember%20SANS%20Overview%20-%20Export%20Control%20-FINAL%20-%2001May23.pdf)). In the 2024 severe case, B-vitamin supplementation and lower cabin carbon dioxide coincided with in-flight improvement, without proof of causation ([Optometry Times, 2024](https://www.optometrytimes.com/view/report-cites-potential-risk-factors-for-severe-spaceflight-associated-neuro-ocular-syndrome)). Disc edema typically resolves 3 to 12 months after return, but globe flattening, hyperopic shift and choroidal folds can become permanent in moderate-to-severe cases and have persisted for up to 12 years ([NASA SANS briefing, 2023](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20230006218/downloads/Brunstetter%20-%20Crewmember%20SANS%20Overview%20-%20Export%20Control%20-FINAL%20-%2001May23.pdf); [SANS expert consensus, Eye 2026](https://link.springer.com/10.1038/s41433-026-04651-6)). Brain changes linger as well: a 3.3% gray-matter reduction about 9 days after flight was still 1.2% at a mean of 209 days in cosmonauts ([Lee et al., 2020](https://link.springer.com/10.1038/s41526-020-0097-9)).

**Ground analog.** In a 30-day strict 6-degree head-down bed rest study, 45% of participants developed SANS-like disc edema, and eight hours of overnight LBNP limited choroid expansion ([SANS expert consensus, Eye 2026](https://link.springer.com/10.1038/s41433-026-04651-6)).

### Balance failures concentrate at landing and scale with duration

Balance shows both a transient and a transition penalty. Space motion sickness affects 60 to 80% of travellers, begins within hours of reaching orbit and usually resolves within two to three days, with a lower incidence in repeat flyers; it was the leading reason for in-flight medication on Shuttle missions, at 47% ([NASA Space Motion Sickness sheet, 2014](https://webcf.waybackmachine.org/web/20211027133343/https://humanresearchroadmap.nasa.gov/Evidence/medicalConditions/Space_Motion_Sickness_%28Space_Adaptation%29.pdf); [Wood, NASA 2021](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20210025094/downloads/Wood-AeroMed%20UTHNeuro-Dec21.pdf); [NASA Behavioral Evidence Report, 2016](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20160004365/downloads/20160004365.pdf)). Treatment is intramuscular promethazine, with intranasal scopolamine at the research stage, plus restricted early activity and a familiar visual orientation ([Wood, NASA 2021](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20210025094/downloads/Wood-AeroMed%20UTHNeuro-Dec21.pdf)).

The larger problem comes at landing and grows with duration. Re-entry motion sickness is put at about 30% after one-to-two-week missions and about 100% after six months, although a 2025 study cites about 15% for short Shuttle flights, and its own landing-day sickness ratings (median 3.27 after short flights, 7.98 after long ones) did not differ significantly ([Wood, NASA 2021](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20210025094/downloads/Wood-AeroMed%20UTHNeuro-Dec21.pdf); [Frontiers in Physiology, 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2025.1677377/full)). That study compared 10 people after 4 to 21 days in space with 36 after 5 to 12 months. On landing day the short group managed 69.5% correct steps on an eyes-open tandem (heel-to-toe) walk and 32.5% with eyes closed; the long group managed **37.6% and 4.2%**. Time to stand and stabilise rose to 2.62 seconds in the short group and 4.24 seconds in the long, and four long-duration participants could not complete some or all tests ([Frontiers in Physiology, 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2025.1677377/full)). About an hour after landing, 54% of 38 crew completed all field-test tasks and 15% completed none. One day after return, eight ISS crew showed deficits in manual dexterity, dual-tasking and tilt perception, with degraded simulated vehicle operation ([Wood, NASA 2021](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20210025094/downloads/Wood-AeroMed%20UTHNeuro-Dec21.pdf)).

All of these are post-landing functional tests: sit-to-stand, tandem walk, walk-and-turn, recovery from fall and a neurological exam. The sources reviewed describe no routine in-flight balance measure and no numeric attention threshold; NASA plots recovery as the share of crew back to 97.5% of their pre-flight score over days 0 to 8 after landing. The same deck lists time constants of 19 hours for short-duration and 111 hours for long-duration crews in a balance comparison, but the slide labelling was ambiguous in extraction and the values need checking against the original ([Wood, NASA 2021](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20210025094/downloads/Wood-AeroMed%20UTHNeuro-Dec21.pdf)). One-year crew are pooled into the 5-to-12-month group, and the 2025 paper's test timing (hours versus days after landing) was internally inconsistent in extraction ([Frontiers in Physiology, 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2025.1677377/full)).

### Immune suppression lasts from launch through month six

Immune changes do not adapt away. In 23 ISS crew sampled before flight, at about flight day 14, in months two to four and at about month six, white-cell and granulocyte counts rose, T-cell activation responses fell and mostly stayed depressed (CD8 responses recovered by late flight), and stimulated cytokine secretion was lower at all three in-flight points; the authors conclude that the alterations persist for the full six months, with the caveat that samples were processed on Earth after delays averaging 37 hours ([Crucian et al., 2015](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1038/npjmgrav.2015.13.pdf)). Natural-killer-cell cytotoxicity declines and can stay impaired for up to 60 days after flight. The disturbance starts before launch: several plasma cytokines were already significantly raised 10 days before launch relative to 180 days before ([Rooney et al., 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6374706)).

Latent herpesviruses are the most trackable consequence. Among 112 astronauts, **53% of Shuttle crew** (10 to 16 days) and **61% of ISS crew** (180 days or more) shed at least one herpesvirus, and each virus was shed by more ISS than Shuttle crew: varicella-zoster 65% versus 41%, Epstein-Barr 96% versus 82%, cytomegalovirus 61% versus 47%. Shedding frequency and amplitude rose with flight duration, Epstein-Barr load ran about tenfold higher in flight, and post-flight shedding lasted up to about 30 days after ISS missions against 3 to 5 days after Shuttle flights ([Rooney et al., 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6374706)). A smaller ISS cohort of nine partly disagrees. Varicella-zoster reactivated in four of its crew, peaking by flight day 90, but Epstein-Barr and herpes simplex showed no association with flight, and salivary cortisol did not change significantly ([Voorhies et al., 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6616552/)). Clinical disease is uncommon: six astronauts reported symptoms linked to reactivation, among them shingles in flight ([Rooney et al., 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6374706)).

The tracked biomarkers are viral DNA by PCR in saliva and urine, salivary cortisol and DHEA and their ratio (which rose in flight in both Shuttle and ISS crews), plasma cytokines and the interferon-gamma to interleukin-4 ratio, natural-killer-cell perforin and granzyme B, T-cell function and anti-cytomegalovirus antibody titres ([Rooney et al., 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6374706)). Blood for the main immune study was returned to Earth for analysis ([Crucian et al., 2015](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1038/npjmgrav.2015.13.pdf)). No viral-load value on which flight surgeons act was found. The gut microbiome shifts in flight and reverts afterwards, while skin microbiome changes persisted after return ([Voorhies et al., 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6616552/)).

The review authors recommend pre-flight vaccination where vaccines exist and call for dedicated countermeasures ([Rooney et al., 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6374706)). A flu vaccine given in orbit during the 340-day mission produced an appropriate response ([NASA Twins Study feature, 2019](https://www.nasa.gov/feature/nasa-s-twins-study-results-published-in-science)). Recovery takes weeks: cytokines returned to pre-flight within about two months ([Voorhies et al., 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6616552/)). Landing is itself an inflammatory event. In the one-year subject, C-reactive protein went from 1 to 19 mg/dL at landing and normalised by 36 days after return ([Garrett-Bakelman et al., 2019](https://lsom.uthscsa.edu/nephrology/wp-content/uploads/sites/38/2019/04/NASA-twin-study_Science.pdf)). No measured immune data exist beyond 12 months of flight, and no numeric Mars projection was found.

### Crews under-eat while vitamin D, temperature and urine chemistry drift

Historically, crews under-eat. NASA's 2005 nutrition standards document records intake averaging about **70% of predicted energy need** from Apollo onward (Skylab crews ate about 100%) and body-mass losses of 1 to 5%, with 10 to 15% also observed ([NASA nutrition standards, 2005](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20200001703/downloads/20200001703.pdf)). Those figures predate the ARED and the current food system, and in 46 ISS astronauts flying between 2011 and 2017 total body mass, lean mass and fat mass were essentially unchanged ([Scott et al., 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41526-023-00256-5)). Under-eating has not disappeared, however: the one-year subject ate about 30% fewer calories than researchers expected and lost 7% of his body mass, which returned after landing ([NASA Twins Study feature, 2019](https://www.nasa.gov/feature/nasa-s-twins-study-results-published-in-science)). The 2005 operating bands classify energy intake of 90 to 100% of requirement as green, 80 to 90% as yellow and below 80% as red ([NASA nutrition standards, 2005](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20200001703/downloads/20200001703.pdf)). Dietary intake and body mass are monitored in flight, since 2016 through the ISS Food Intake Tracker tablet app with weekly downlink, and every ISS astronaut completes a biochemical nutritional assessment before and after flight ([NASA nutrition page, 2026](https://www.nasa.gov/?p=209742)).

Vitamin D falls because astronauts cannot synthesise it without sunlight ([NASA nutrition page, 2026](https://www.nasa.gov/?p=209742)). Mir crews had 25-hydroxyvitamin D 32 to 36% lower during and after three-to-four-month missions, and 11 ISS astronauts on four-to-six-month missions showed a fall even with supplements, along with urinary magnesium down about 45% and haematocrit and haemoglobin down 2 to 4% ([NASA nutrition standards, 2005](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20200001703/downloads/20200001703.pdf); [Zwart et al., 2009](https://scispace.com/pdf/nutritional-status-assessment-before-during-and-after-long-nlopcqirbm.pdf)). The two NASA documents consulted state vitamin D ranges in different units, a 2005 operating band of 25 to 138 nmol/L and a 2026 target of 35 to 90 ng/mL ([NASA nutrition standards, 2005](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20200001703/downloads/20200001703.pdf); [NASA OCHMO Bone Loss brief, 2026](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ochmo-tb-030-bone-loss.pdf)). Sodium intake on the ISS exceeded 4.5 g per day against a requirement of 1.5 to 2.3 g ([NASA nutrition standards, 2005](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20200001703/downloads/20200001703.pdf)).

Three slower metabolic drifts are documented. An insulin-resistance index rose during six-month flights in eight astronauts (P < 0.001), more in men; the abstract gives no index values ([Hughson et al., 2016, abstract record](https://dissem.in/p/70048131/increased-postflight-carotid-artery-stiffness-and-inflight-insulin-resistance-resulting-from-6-mo-spaceflight-in-male-and-female-astronauts)). Resting core body temperature rose by about **1 °C, developing gradually over 2.5 months**, and exercise core temperature often exceeded 40 °C; it was measured with a forehead sensor combining skin temperature and heat flux ([Stahn et al., 2017, DLR record](https://elib.dlr.de/116228); [Sci.News summary](https://www.sci.news/space/weightlessness-body-temperature-05609.html)). Urine chemistry shifts toward stone formation in flight as bone loss raises urinary calcium and crews run dehydrated, though clinical stones present mostly afterwards: 32 post-flight stones had been reported in US astronauts by mid-2016 against one documented in-flight case, in a Russian cosmonaut, and it is unclear whether flight raises symptomatic incidence above the general population's ([National Academies, 2018](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/24953/chapter/8)).

The 2005 standards set urinary limits of oxalate no more than 62 mg/day, citrate above 160 mg/day, pH no more than 8 and supersaturation no more than 3 for the main stone salts, and a whole-blood glucose band of 70 to 105 mg/dL; the calcium limit (375 mg/day) and minimum urine volume (1 L/day) were machine-extracted and need checking against the original table ([NASA nutrition standards, 2005](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20200001703/downloads/20200001703.pdf)). Potassium citrate is the only approved operational flight countermeasure for stones, alongside hydration, diet and the bone countermeasures, and diagnostic ultrasound is available in flight ([National Academies, 2018](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/24953/chapter/8)).

### Crews sleep six hours against an 8.5-hour schedule

Sleep is short from before launch and stays short. Across 64 Shuttle and 21 ISS astronauts and more than 4,200 nights in space, crews scheduled for 8.5 hours averaged **5.96 hours on Shuttle and 6.09 hours on the ISS**, having already averaged under 6.5 hours in the training period about three months before launch. Only 12% of Shuttle and 24% of ISS sleep episodes reached seven hours, against 42% and 50% once home ([Barger et al., 2014, press release](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/595450)). A later ISS study of 24 astronauts found a mean of 6.5 hours, 34.9% of nights at six hours or less (40.0% on weekdays, 22.5% on weekends) and no change across mission quarters ([Jones et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8919197)). In these data the deficit is a steady state tied to the work schedule; it does not deepen with months in orbit.

Short nights have measurable costs. After nights of six hours or less, reaction speed was significantly slower, and nights under six hours went with higher stress, mental fatigue and sleepiness ratings ([Jones et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8919197)). Estimated circadian phase fell outside the sleep period on 19% of days in 21 ISS astronauts, and misaligned sleep episodes were about an hour shorter ([NASA Sleep Evidence Report, 2016](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20160003864/downloads/20160003864.pdf)). Medication is widespread: 78% of Shuttle crew used sleep drugs, on 52% of nights, about three quarters of ISS crew used them at some point, and the drugs shortened sleep onset without lengthening sleep ([Barger et al., 2014, press release](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/595450); [NASA Sleep Evidence Report, 2016](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20160003864/downloads/20160003864.pdf)).

Sleep in these studies was measured with wrist actigraphy and sleep logs, although one of the research notes could not confirm the method in the press release that carries the headline figures; the Reaction Self Test adds a brief vigilance test with self-ratings of sleep, stress, workload and fatigue every fourth day ([Jones et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8919197)). NASA-STD-3001 recommends eight hours of sleep, and fitness-for-duty guidance defines critical overload as 10-hour days on more than three days a week or more than 60 hours a week ([NASA Sleep Evidence Report, 2016](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20160003864/downloads/20160003864.pdf)). No published numeric trigger for intervention on sleep duration was found. Countermeasures are private sleep quarters with personal control of light, noise, temperature and airflow, tunable solid-state lighting, schedule adjustment by flight surgeons, and hypnotics ([NASA OCHMO Behavioral Health brief, 2023](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ochmo-tb-016-behavioral-health.pdf); [NASA Sleep Evidence Report, 2016](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20160003864/downloads/20160003864.pdf)). Sleep lengthens after landing ([Jones et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8919197)).

**Ground laboratory.** Performance on emergency awakening was worse after zolpidem or zaleplon than after placebo, and restricting sleep to five to six hours impaired performance within a week ([NASA Sleep Evidence Report, 2016](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20160003864/downloads/20160003864.pdf)).

**Ground analog.** Confinement without an orbital workload runs the other way: in the 520-day Mars-500 simulation, sleep averaged about 7.4 hours by actigraphy and increased over the mission ([Basner et al., 2013](https://www.med.upenn.edu/uep/assets/user-content/documents/PNAS2013Basner1212646110withSupportingAppendix.pdf)).

## Mood and cognition hold for six months; analog crews fray later

### Symptoms are common in flight; diagnoses and emergencies are not

Reported in-flight problems are mostly sub-clinical symptoms. NASA's 2016 evidence report counts 34 behavioral signs and symptoms among 208 Shuttle crew members (0.11 per 14-day mission, with anxiety and annoyance the most frequent), 24 instances of anxiety and 4 astronauts with depression over 28.84 person-years (**0.83 and 0.14 per person-year**), and depressive symptoms in 2 of 7 NASA astronauts on Mir (0.77 per person-year). The same report states that US spaceflight has had no behavioral emergency and no formal in-flight diagnosis of anxiety or depression, and that across 41 ISS expeditions only one event, the unexpected death of an astronaut's parent, was judged to have possibly affected a mission. Three Soviet and Russian missions (Soyuz-21, Soyuz T-14 and Soyuz TM-2) ended early with psychological factors cited, and cosmonaut neurasthenia is described as unlikely on flights shorter than four months ([NASA Behavioral Evidence Report, 2016](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20160004365/downloads/20160004365.pdf)). In journals kept by 10 ISS astronauts, interpersonal conflict was rare ([Stuster, 2010](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20100026549/downloads/20100026549.pdf)).

Several of these numbers carry flagged problems. The Shuttle rate converts arithmetically to about 2.87 per person-year, but the extracted text read "one per 2.87 person-years", and the report's text and table disagree on whether the data cover 1981 to 1989 or 1981 to 1998, so the figure needs checking against the PDF before use. All rates come from the 2016 edition because a 2025 update could not be opened ([NASA Behavioral Evidence Report, 2025](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20250001454/downloads/BMed%20Evidence%20Report%20final%202-10-25.docx)). No per-person-year rates were found for ISS expeditions after 2014.

**Projection.** NASA's Integrated Medical Model assigns far lower rates to diagnosable disorders than the observed symptom rates, 0.0019 to 0.0071 per person-year for anxiety and 0.0029 to 0.0036 for depression. The report's table and text give different probabilities over 120 person-years (for example 57% versus 85.2% for anxiety in women) and do not reconcile them ([NASA Behavioral Evidence Report, 2016](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20160004365/downloads/20160004365.pdf)).

### Stress climbs by quarter while cognitive speed dips early

Measured ISS data show stability with drift. Stress ratings peaked about a week before launch, when reaction speed was also at its slowest ([Jones et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8919197)). About 29 days into flight, sustained attention was roughly half a standard deviation slower than before flight; processing speed ran about 0.7 to 0.8 standard deviations slower across the flight and working-memory speed 0.8 to 1.2 slower in later phases, all with accuracy intact. Over a six-month mission the same 25 astronauts showed no systematic cognitive decline; **11.8% of in-flight and post-flight scores** fell 1.5 standard deviations or more below the individual's own pre-flight mean, and risk tolerance was lower about 30 days before return and after landing ([Dev et al., 2024](https://frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2024.1451269/pdf)). Across mission quarters in 24 astronauts, self-rated stress rose (the fourth quarter higher than the first and second), caffeine use increased, workload ratings and sleep stayed flat, and reaction speed improved slightly ([Jones et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8919197)). Standardised mood and social-climate scales in 30 Mir and ISS crew on four-to-seven-month missions showed no significant change between quarters ([Kanas et al., 2021](https://asma.kglmeridian.com/downloadpdf/view/journals/amhp/92/8/article-p689.pdf)). Journal entries tell a slightly darker story: negative comments trended upward, naps were reported more in the third and fourth quarters, and crews described switching from counting up to counting down around month four ([NASA Sleep Evidence Report, 2016](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20160003864/downloads/20160003864.pdf); [Stuster, 2010](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20100026549/downloads/20100026549.pdf)).

For a full year in flight there is one subject. His cognition was stable in orbit; speed and accuracy then declined after landing and stayed down for six months ([NASA Twins Study feature, 2019](https://www.nasa.gov/feature/nasa-s-twins-study-results-published-in-science)).

### The third-quarter morale dip remains unproven

The popular idea of a third-quarter dip in morale is not established. Support comes from descriptive sources: 14 of 20 astronaut journals showed a third-quarter drop in net positivity, judged by visual inspection without statistical tests, and HI-SEAS analog crews showed adverse changes after the halfway point without the expected end-of-mission rebound ([Kanas et al., 2021](https://asma.kglmeridian.com/downloadpdf/view/journals/amhp/92/8/article-p689.pdf); [Rosenberg et al., 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2022.898841/full)). Quantitative studies mostly find nothing: the Mir and ISS mood scales, the Mars-500 simulation, the ISS Reaction Self Test data, a review of 26 reports (13 with no change in negative emotion over time) and a review of 72 analog sources ([Kanas et al., 2021](https://asma.kglmeridian.com/downloadpdf/view/journals/amhp/92/8/article-p689.pdf); [Basner et al., 2014](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0093298); [Jones et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8919197); [Bell et al., 2019](https://frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.00811/pdf)). NASA's own evidence report calls the pattern "somewhat statistically inconsistent", and the field's leading authors concluded in 2021 that there is "little evidence" for a time-driven effect ([NASA Behavioral Evidence Report, 2016](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20160004365/downloads/20160004365.pdf); [Kanas et al., 2021](https://asma.kglmeridian.com/downloadpdf/view/journals/amhp/92/8/article-p689.pdf)). Several datasets instead show stress or conflict rising toward the end of the mission.

### Social strain shows up in ground analogs lasting 8 to 17 months

**Ground analogs.** Everything in this subsection comes from isolation studies on Earth. Over the 520-day Mars-500 simulation (six crew), active waking time fell 7.0% and rest time rose 50.4% from the first to the last quarter, vigilance did not deteriorate, crew-average depression and mood-disturbance scores stayed low, and about 55% of the variance was stable difference between individuals that was visible from early in the mission. One of six reported depressive symptoms (the paper's abstract and body give different frequencies), two members accounted for 85% of reported conflicts, and conflicts with mission control outnumbered those within the crew 41 to 8. Four of the six had at least one sleep-wake problem ([Basner et al., 2013](https://www.med.upenn.edu/uep/assets/user-content/documents/PNAS2013Basner1212646110withSupportingAppendix.pdf); [Basner et al., 2014](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0093298)).

In 12 people wintering about nine months at Concordia station in Antarctica, loneliness and conflict rose, cohesion and self-rated performance fell, and contacts clustered increasingly by nationality; the paper's own figure and discussion disagree on whether daily contact time fell or rose ([Cantisani et al., 2026](https://www.isi.it/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/cantisani-et-al-2026-social-interactions-in-isolated-confined-and-extreme-environments-a-study-of-antarctic-winter-1.pdf)). In the 120-day SIRIUS-19 simulation, crew audio sessions with mission control fell from 320 in the first 10 days to 34 in the last 10 ([Supolkina et al., 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2021.751170/full)). HI-SEAS crews on 8-to-12-month missions showed stress markers peaking in month one and near the midpoint, perceived stress rising through months five to seven, and inactivity peaking at months six to seven ([Rosenberg et al., 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2022.898841/full)). A review of 72 analog sources found that every team with data had reported at least one conflict by about 40% of mission elapsed (at least 90 days, as the review reports it) ([Bell et al., 2019](https://frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.00811/pdf)). A 240-day isolation mission, compared with 45-day missions, showed subtle stress increases and a continuing decline in perceived emotional support and psychological safety; NASA's summary gives no numeric values ([Bell et al., NASA 2023](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20230001743/downloads/HFBP-EM%20in%20HERA%20C6%20IWS%202023.pdf)).

Polar expeditions supply an incidence estimate: about 5% of expeditioners develop symptoms severe enough to warrant medication or therapy, according to a university summary of a Lancet review ([USC release, 2007](https://dworakpeck.usc.edu/news/polar-expeditions-take-toll-on-mental-health)). NASA's 378-day CHAPEA missions collect behavioral data but have published no results ([NASA CHAPEA page, 2026](https://www.nasa.gov/humans-in-space/chapea/)). No measured behavioral data exist beyond 520 days of ground isolation ([NASA Behavioral Evidence Report, 2016](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20160004365/downloads/20160004365.pdf)).

### Monitoring relies on biweekly conferences and monthly cognitive screens

Operational monitoring is sparse and clinician-led. Every ISS crew member has a private psychological conference of 15 to 20 minutes every two weeks, a computerised cognitive screen (WinSCAT) about every 30 days that is compared with a personal pre-flight baseline, and weekly private medical conferences with the flight surgeon ([Beven, 2017](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20170003866/downloads/20170003866.pdf); [NASA Behavioral Evidence Report, 2016](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20160004365/downloads/20160004365.pdf); [Williams et al., NASA](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20180003970/downloads/20180003970.pdf)). Sources differ on WinSCAT's length, 30 minutes in one and 11 to 15 minutes in another. Research studies add denser instruments: the Reaction Self Test (a brief vigilance test with self-ratings, morning and evening every fourth day, with 78.9% of scheduled tests completed), the 10-test Cognition battery, wrist actigraphy, confidential journals and mood questionnaires ([Jones et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8919197); [Dev et al., 2024](https://frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2024.1451269/pdf); [Stuster, 2010](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20100026549/downloads/20100026549.pdf)). Analog studies have used weekly mood and depression inventories, conflict questionnaires, wearable proximity sensors, voice and facial-expression analysis and hair cortisol ([Basner et al., 2014](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0093298); [Cantisani et al., 2026](https://www.isi.it/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/cantisani-et-al-2026-social-interactions-in-isolated-confined-and-extreme-environments-a-study-of-antarctic-winter-1.pdf); [Supolkina et al., 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2021.751170/full); [Rosenberg et al., 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2022.898841/full)).

No public source gives a numeric cut-off that triggers intervention. NASA's documents describe clinical judgement against each person's baseline ([NASA OCHMO Behavioral Health brief, 2023](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ochmo-tb-016-behavioral-health.pdf); [Beven, 2017](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20170003866/downloads/20170003866.pdf)). The nearest thing to a threshold is the research definition of a low score, 1.5 standard deviations or more below the individual's pre-flight mean ([Dev et al., 2024](https://frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2024.1451269/pdf)). NASA-STD-3001 does set workload limits (a Bedford rating of 3 or less for nominal tasks and 6 or less for degraded ones), calls for extra cognitive testing after an on-orbit injury or illness, and says missions beyond six months or beyond low Earth orbit should add minimally obtrusive objective measures of psychological state. The signs NASA's brief names are despondence, social withdrawal and possible hallucinations, with sleep, workload, medication side effects, hypoxia, nutrition and carbon dioxide as contributing factors ([NASA OCHMO Behavioral Health brief, 2023](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ochmo-tb-016-behavioral-health.pdf)). In analog data, the patterns that accompanied individual difficulty were persistent sleep-onset insomnia with vigilance lapses, free-running or daytime-shifted sleep, sustained depressive-symptom reports, a disproportionate share of conflicts, falling crew-to-ground communication and nationality-based subgrouping ([Basner et al., 2014](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0093298); [Supolkina et al., 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2021.751170/full); [Cantisani et al., 2026](https://www.isi.it/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/cantisani-et-al-2026-social-interactions-in-isolated-confined-and-extreme-environments-a-study-of-antarctic-winter-1.pdf)).

### Countermeasures are judged adequate only for six-month missions

NASA's package is select-out psychiatric screening, pre-flight training in stress management, conflict management and team skills, in-flight psychological and family conferences, care packages, work-hour and sleep standards, private crew quarters, circadian lighting, and an onboard kit holding two anxiolytics, two antidepressants, two antipsychotics and sedatives ([NASA Behavioral Evidence Report, 2016](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20160004365/downloads/20160004365.pdf); [NASA OCHMO Behavioral Health brief, 2023](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ochmo-tb-016-behavioral-health.pdf)). No effect sizes were found for any of these. NASA treats the absence of behavioral emergencies as indirect evidence that they work for missions of about six months, and its 2023 brief calls for more family resources and improved crew-ground communication for missions beyond six months or beyond low Earth orbit ([NASA Behavioral Evidence Report, 2016](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20160004365/downloads/20160004365.pdf); [NASA OCHMO Behavioral Health brief, 2023](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ochmo-tb-016-behavioral-health.pdf)). Post-flight behavioral assessments are scheduled at 3, 14 and 30 to 45 days after return ([Beven, 2017](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20170003866/downloads/20170003866.pdf)).

## Beyond six months the evidence rests on one or two people

### The one-year flight shows partial levelling, not doubling

The longest well-instrumented flight is the 340-day Twins Study mission, a single subject with his identical twin as ground control, and its authors call it hypothesis-generating. Its pattern is partial levelling: bone turnover was 50 to 60% above baseline in the first half-year and lower in the second, and carotid wall thickening did not progress in months 7 to 12 ([Garrett-Bakelman et al., 2019](https://lsom.uthscsa.edu/nephrology/wp-content/uploads/sites/38/2019/04/NASA-twin-study_Science.pdf)). Eye changes in two one-year subjects resembled those of six-month crews, yet some processes were still moving late: optic rim width kept increasing to day 270, and most epigenetic changes occurred in the second six months ([Macias et al., 2021, repository record](https://scholarlycommons.henryford.com/surgery_articles/493); [NASA Twins Study feature, 2019](https://www.nasa.gov/feature/nasa-s-twins-study-results-published-in-science)). After return, body weight, the gut microbiome and **91.3% of gene-expression changes** reverted within six months, while carotid thickening, ocular changes, a subset of immune and DNA-repair gene expression, and reduced cognitive speed and accuracy were still present at the end of the study. The two summaries consulted differ on telomeres, which lengthened in flight and shortened after return: one reports them back to normal about six months later, while NASA's feature lists telomere dynamics among the measures still altered ([NASA Twins Study feature, 2019](https://www.nasa.gov/feature/nasa-s-twins-study-results-published-in-science); [ScienceDaily, 2019](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2019/04/190411165113.htm)).

Longer flights have added duration but no published physiology. Frank Rubio's 371 days (2022 to 2023) is the US record and Valeri Polyakov's 437 days the world record; a news report says Rubio was carried from the capsule and estimated two to six months before feeling fully normal ([Live Science, 2023](https://www.livescience.com/space/space-exploration/record-breaking-astronaut-frank-rubio-finally-returns-to-earth-after-accidentally-spending-371-days-in-space)). No peer-reviewed bone, muscle or cardiovascular data were found for either flight, nor group results for other one-year-class flights. NASA's CIPHER study is taking measurements on missions from weeks to one year for the stated purpose of extrapolating to multi-year flights ([NASA bone and muscle page, 2023](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/station/iss-research/counteracting-bone-and-muscle-loss-in-microgravity/)).

### Communication delay strips out real-time support

Distance changes the support system as much as the physiology. A 50-second one-way delay imposed on ISS tasks with three astronauts proved feasible and was associated with performance and mood challenges; the detailed outcomes are in a separate paper that was not retrieved ([Kintz and Palinkas, 2016](https://asma.kglmeridian.com/downloadpdf/view/journals/amhp/87/11/article-p940.pdf)). NASA investigators summarise prior studies as linking delays of 50 seconds or more to more stress and frustration, slower and more error-prone task work and lower cohesion ([Holden et al., 2026](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20260002621/downloads/Holden%20et%20al%202026%20-%20comm%20delay%20study%20methodsa.pdf)). The supports that keep the six-month ISS rating acceptable (real-time conferences every two weeks, family calls, resupplied care packages) are the ones a Mars-scale delay degrades. A 2015 NASA Inspector General report found that the agency had no validated mitigation strategy for Mars-mission behavioral risk, and the Human Research Program's rating rises with distance through consequence, from 2 to 3 to 4, while likelihood stays at 3 ([NASA Behavioral Evidence Report, 2016](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20160004365/downloads/20160004365.pdf); [NASA Human Research Roadmap](https://humanresearchroadmap.nasa.gov/Risks/risk.aspx?i=99)).

## Conclusion

The pre-flight baseline, against which nearly every NASA limit is defined, is itself a disturbed state. Crews are already sleeping under 6.5 hours three months before launch, self-rated stress peaks and reaction speed bottoms out in the final pre-launch week, and plasma cytokines are raised 10 days before launch. A "percent of baseline" reading therefore depends on when the baseline was taken, a point the sources document but do not resolve.

The second lesson is a mismatch between what is regulated and what is observable. The firmest numeric standards (bone density, muscle strength) refer to tests that exist only on the ground, while the quantities easiest to read in orbit mislead: resting blood pressure moves in a direction that looks healthy, and whole-body mass holds steady while calf muscle and trabecular bone erode. The informative in-flight signals are the scheduled, effortful ones: the monthly peak-exercise test, the retinal scans, vascular ultrasound and repeated reaction testing. For eyes, immunity, balance and behavior the sources give few or no numeric action thresholds at all. Averages also hide the people at risk: on identical hardware roughly one crew member in seven loses a fifth of leg power or aerobic capacity, and in the longest isolation study two of six people accounted for 85% of reported conflicts. Finally, the evidence horizon is short. The notes found no published physiological data for flights beyond about one year and no behavioral data beyond 520 days of ground isolation, and NASA lists the effects of partial gravity on bone and orthostatic tolerance as unknown, so every multi-year figure in this report is an extrapolation awaiting data.
